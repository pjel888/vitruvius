###### Message from the Secretary

![image 8](Open Space Development Guidelines_images/imageFile8.png)

Open spaces are more than just undeveloped land; they are the lungs of our cities, the playgrounds for our children, the sanctuaries for our biodiversity, and the vital arteries that support our well-being. They provide critical ecosystem services, from clean air and water to flood control and climate change mitigation. They are essential for physical activity, mental rejuvenation, social interaction, and cultural expression. In a rapidly urbanizing landscape, the preservation and thoughtful development of these invaluable assets become paramount in the aspect of land use and human settlements planning.

As the primary national government entity responsible for the management of housing, human settlement and urban development in the country, it is my pleasure to present the Department of Human Settlement and Urban Development’s Open Space Development Guidelines, which aims to mainstream the open space integration in the land use planning process, guide open space development planning of local government units (LGUs), and establish a synergized classification system of open spaces.

The development of this policy is part of the DHSUD’s PLANADO (Plan & Do) Program, which is a comprehensive approach to building the capacity of LGUs and other urban actors to formulate and implement land use, physical, and urban development plans and programs. By leveraging the Comprehensive Land Use Plan and Zoning Ordinance as a powerful and strategic tool for local development, this Guidelines will help in the preservation, conservation, regeneration, and creation of open spaces.

I wish to thank the Government of Japan, the Global Facility for Disaster Reduction and Recovery, and the World Bank for their support in the formulation of this important Guidelines. I am also grateful to the relevant national government agencies (NGAs), professional organizations, LGUs, and other stakeholders who provided valuable inputs in the development of this Guidelines.

The successful implementation of this Guidelines necessitates the collective effort and commitment of all stakeholders. National and local government units, private sector partners, civil society organizations, academic institutions, and every Filipino citizen are urged to embrace and champion the principles outlined herein. Through collaborative action, our communities can be transformed into models of green, livable, and resilient spaces.

Ultimately, this Guidelines aim to foster a Philippines characterized by cleaner air, greener environments, and thriving communities that benefit from well-planned open spaces.

ENGR. JOSE RAMON P. ALILING Secretary Department of Human Settlements and Urban Development (DHSUD)

###### Foreword

Many environmental advantages can be derived from open spaces, including enhanced biodiversity, better air quality, carbon sequestration, nature and culture appreciation, and improved water management. Moreover, open spaces also mitigate flood risks, curb pollution, regulate microclimates, and provide essential ecosystem services. Economically and aesthetically, these spaces cool urban environments, diminish the reliance on air-conditioning, and elevate property values. This is evident in the real estate developments near Iloilo City’s River Esplanade, as well as in central business and residential districts like Bonifacio Global City and the Makati Central Business District, which boasts of verdant, walkable corridors and diverse outdoor recreational amenities and parks. Among these are Ayala Triangle Gardens, Burgos Park, Legaspi Park, Washington Sycip Park, and BGC Greenway Park.

Understanding that open spaces are fundamental to enhancing the quality of life, the government is prioritizing the development of comprehensive guidelines to support local government units (LGUs) in creating and maintaining these essential areas as part of its sustainable urban solutions.

The significance of open spaces rises as disaster and climate risk factors for human settlements intensify. These areas are currently a major area of emphasis for national planning since they have become essential for minimizing environmental effects. Future improvements in the general health and well-being of the Filipino people can also be foreseen with a nationwide strategy for the creation of open spaces.

Nevertheless, the goal of the Open Space Development Guidelines is to offer recommendations for the creation and enhancement of open spaces by LGUs. To address issues related to climate change, disasters, and urbanization, this guide will be incorporated into the nation’s planning frameworks, more particularly, LGUs’ comprehensive land use plans.

The guidelines offer strategies along with an assessment of the state of open spaces, both domestically and globally. It aims to integrate open space development into comprehensive land-use plans and mainstream its implementation and protection. Future updates will include digital planning processes to strengthen land use plans with an ultimate goal of ensuring the Filipino’s access to livable communities.

## Introduction

# 1 I

- A. SITUATIONAL OVERVIEW


The Benefits of Open Spaces

Open spaces are essential for sustainable city development and community well-being. As cities grow, the need for these spaces increases. Globally, developing open spaces has become a trend (Nabila et.al., 2023) because they improve the quality of life and benefit both man and nature. In the Philippines, open spaces help achieve sustainable development goals (SDGs) as natural systems reduce disaster risks and adapt to climate change (Uy and Tapnio, 2021).

As more Filipinos appreciate open spaces, open space value in spatial planning increases (Dewi et al., 2018; Rakhshandehroo et al., 2014; Haq, 2011). This growing appreciation for open spaces also provides urban development gains in the form of environmental, economic, aesthetic, social, and physical benefits.

|Environmental Benefits: Open spaces improve air quality, support wildlife, manage water, prevent floods, and control pollution. They also help keep the climate balanced and protect nature. It also offers some of the important ecosystem services such as: (1) regulating services including climate regulation and air quality improvement; (2) provisioning services like food and water supply; (3) supporting services like soil formation and nutrient cycling; and (4) cultural services such as recreation and aesthetics (Nabila et.al., 2023).<br><br>Economic and Aesthetic Benefits: Open spaces cool down cities, reducing the need for air conditioning, and making areas more attractive. As a result, open spaces also help cities by increasing property/land values.<br><br>Social and Physical Benefits: Open spaces are great for our health and well-being. They provide places for exercise and relaxation, attract tourists, and boost the local economy. They also help build stronger communities by giving people places to gather and connect.|
|---|


The Philippines’ Open Spaces Situation and Challenges

The development of open spaces in the Philippines has become a crucial aspect of urban planning and sustainable development in recent years (National Economic and Development Authority, 2020). These spaces not only contribute to the aesthetic and recreational value of a city (Alcazaren, 2019) but also play a vital role in mitigating the environmental impacts of urbanization (National Economic and Development Authority, 2020) and aid in enhancing the overall health and well-being of the population (United Nations Development Programme, 2021).

|OPEN SPACES SITUATION IN THE 1900s<br><br>Saloma and Akpedonu (2021) emphasized the significance of public open spaces in the Philippines, especially in Manila and Quezon City. They pointed out how these areas were integral to historical urban planning efforts, including Burnham’s Plan and the Frost-Arellano Plan. In the early 1900s, these spaces were cherished for fostering inclusivity among various social classes. However, they also observed that access to these areas was frequently restricted by social class, with numerous proposed parks either remaining unconstructed or being transformed into exclusive recreational facilities, with the Intramuros Golf Course being cited as an example.<br><br>In the next era of governance, open spaces became less important compared to housing needs. Saloma and Akpedonu (2021) observed this in Quezon City, Philippines, which was originally planned to meet the housing needs of the common people. Inspired by the City Beautiful plans of Manila and the 1898 Garden City concept by Ebenezer Howard, Quezon City developed new ideologies for open spaces. These ideas combined urban and rural elements, providing access to both city services and natural environments similar to communities on the fringes of Manila. Public parks in Quezon City were mainly created for the working class living in the city’s housing units, rather than for different social classes (Saloma and Akpedonu, 2021).<br><br>Figure 1. The government has given LGUs the freedom to plan and improve public areas in their regions. Other than the LGUs, government offices are created to focus on park management, like the National Parks Development Committee (NPDC) that manages Rizal Park and Paco Park in Manila. Some non-profit or civil society organizations also work with the government to manage parks. These changes have led to a mix of public, private, and public-private open spaces in the urban landscape.

Besides the challenges in creating open spaces, the Philippines also needs to address natural disasters and climate change issues. The country is the fourth most affected by climate change impacts, according to the 2021 Global Climate Risk Index (Philippine Department of Human Settlements and Urban Development, 2023). Due to its location, the Philippines is at high risk of severe floods, typhoons, and earthquakes directly affect the prioritization of open spaces. It is then important for sustainable urban development to not just plan open spaces, but consider current and future risks and vulnerability related to natural hazards in planning these. Integrating studies on open spaces can help make communities more resilient and support the well-being of the environment in the future.

Over the years, open space development in the Philippines has remained a planning challenge, even though it was considered in earlier plans. This challenge is due to the uneven distribution of land compared to the population size of Philippine cities. Saloma and Akpedonu (2021) noted that Manila has only 0.03 percent open space per person, which is far below the World Health Organization’s recommendation of 9 square meters per person and a green

space within a 10-minute walk (Saloma & Akpedonu, 2021). They then concluded a failure in the attempts to include open spaces in early city plans. They also pointed out that these plans did not encourage different social classes to mix. Wealthy people prefer private open spaces like resorts, golf courses, and farms, which eventually led to gentrification. In the Philippines, quality, accessible public open spaces are still lacking. Unfortunately, those who can afford to maintain these open spaces see less value for protection (Saloma & Akpedonu, 2021).

###### The Need to Mainstream Open Spaces in Comprehensive Local Government Plans

While open spaces offer many benefits, the government saw the vitality of integrating them into the local government’s comprehensive land use plans. To be able to positively reinforce its integration into the local plans, several actors must be added to the framework of open spaces integration, ranging from the government to the private sector, currently joining in the open space development conversations.

|ASIA PACIFIC OPEN SPACE SITUATIONS<br><br>Indonesia’s Open Space Streamlining through Law No. 26 of 2007<br><br>In Indonesia, Law No. 26 of 2007 (Sihombing and Hutapea, 2022; Werner, 2014) requires 30% of urban areas to be open spaces, with 20% public and 10% private. This law is supported by guidelines from the Minister of Public Works, which outline the functions of open spaces such as (1) Intrinsic Functions: Act as city lungs, regulate microclimate, provide shade, produce oxygen, absorb rainwater, offer animal habitats, create windbreaks, and absorb pollutants; and (2) Extrinsic Functions: Support recreation and education, promote urban agriculture, beautify the city, and boost creativity and productivity.<br><br>Malaysia and Singapore’s Green Open Space Policies<br><br>An analysis of Malaysia and Singaporeʼs green open space policies to improve urban resilience to climate change was done by Sabri et al. (2023). The paper studied and compared how effective the open spaces policies of Malaysia and Singapore were in terms of urban resilience promotion using small urban parks as examples. After a comprehensive literature search through online portals and official government websites was conducted, they found out that urban green space strategies were already in place in both countries. Malaysia and Singapore have a clear vision of green space management as shown by active policy frameworks, programs, recognition of the economic value of green projects, and overall maintenance. Significant differences include limited funding in Malaysia versus more budget allocation in Singapore and the extent of awareness by its citizens. Although it showed how Singapore as a model is more sustainably effective and robust, Malaysia is not far from achieving its goal as long as a proactive approach is taken by the government in terms of fund allocation for green space management and consistent promotion of green policies to fulfill the 10 percent open space mandate.<br><br>Figure 3. However, the protection and enhancement of open spaces still entail concerted efforts to be able to be successfully implemented. Globally, countries are pressed into ensuring that systematic open space planning is done to foster a greener future for our living environments. Relatively, open spaces should be established not just for its ecological and social benefits but also because of its purpose in sustaining both the biodiversity and cultural hearths of cities.

The presence and spatial size of open spaces are also being threatened by different drivers of change anchored mostly on urbanization. As a result of this challenge, there are rising efforts to protect and enhance open spaces globally as countries are slowly recognizing their value for sustainable development, human well-being, and climate and disaster resilience to become livable and resilient cities. Unfortunately, the present open spaces in the Asia Pacific are still relatively lower than needed.

One major challenge is the difficulty in implementing open spaces. According to Byrne and Sipe (2010), different communities need different types of open spaces, making it hard to meet everyone’s needs. In densely populated areas, most remaining open spaces are already used for other purposes.

###### B. RATIONALE

Despite the pressing challenges of access and usage of open spaces, there is still good potential for the Philippines to carry on with the initial ideologies of open spaces provisions in our cities. The government, first and foremost, is motivated to deliver a plan for integrating open spaces into most of the country’s policies through the project, PlanSmart for Sustainable Human Settlements. In this initiative, new efforts to synergize through a nationwide approach are underway and can be implemented through an Open Space Development Guidelines.

In more recent years, the public open space development program called the Green, Green, Green (GGG) Program has funded and implemented 391 projects from 2018 to 2021 which has assisted LGUs in making their communities more livable, sustainable, and well-connected through the development or enhancement of public open spaces and green infrastructure projects (Department of Budget and Management, 2024). Initially called the Local Government Support Fund - Assistance to Cities (LGSF-AC), which was first launched in 2017, the GGG Program has been continued in 2024 as provided for in the FY 2024 General Appropriations Act (RA No. 11975), and operationalized in DBM LBC No. 158, dated July 11, 2024.

The GGG program is part of the current Marcos administration’s national infrastructure development program, the Build Better More Program, which provides local government units (LGUs) with financial and technical assistance to develop and implement green infrastructure projects, which includes esplanades, parks, arboretum, botanical gardens, bike lanes, walkways, and other active mobility projects. These spaces contribute to the reduction of the urban heatisland effect and pollution, improvement of environmental quality, and establishment of more livable communities in the country.

These initiatives proved to be crucial in promoting a proactive focus on nature-based solutions for prevention, mitigation, and rehabilitation of the landscape (Uy and Tapnio, 2021). Moreover, placemaking as an essential component of DBM LBC No. 158 in the context of “Designing with People” highlights the importance of participatory planning at the local level.

DHSUD’s Open Space Development Guidelines will further assist LGUs in the implementation of more open space development projects, programs, and activities (PPAs) in the country, providing a comprehensive list of strategies and examples that can be implemented to achieve the nationwide goals of these government-led programs.

At the national level, DHSUD has also guided LGUs on how to prioritize Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) and climate change actions in their comprehensive land use plans to create more resilient communities. In this light, DHSUD also began a review of national policies and frameworks related to DRR and climate change to be able to comprehensively find gaps that can be improved to address this concern.

Moreover, there are recent policies that promote open spaces development strategies in the Philippines, such as the Joint Administrative Order No. 2023-0001 (JAO 2023-0001) of the DOH-DILG-DOT-DENR-DHSUD-DPWH-PCWNCCA. JAO 2023-0001 contains the guidelines on the creation, use, and management of parks and public open spaces for the promotion of the physical and mental health and social well-being in the country. With the enforcement of JAO 2023-0001 in the country, public open spaces are again showcased as important areas of livable communities.

Furthermore, cities nationwide began their implementation of PPAs related to open space development. For example, the Baguio City Council has recently approved an ordinance highlighting the importance of creating, using, and maintaining healthy open spaces like parks. Known as Ordinance 93 or the Healthy Public Open Spaces Initiative Ordinance of the City of Baguio, this initiative aims to ensure the development and upkeep of public open spaces within the city. Among its main features are the stipulations that these spaces should cater to the needs of children, elderly individuals, persons with disabilities, and pets, as well as provide a safe environment for individuals of all sexual orientations and gender identities (Fokno, 2023).

Bayawan City in Negros has also been on top of creating its own Open Spaces Development Guidelines following examples and strategies from global and local policies and strategies (City Government of Bayawan, 2022).

The municipality of Palanan, Isabela, also provided Municipal Ordinance No. 2023-21-142, which particularly focused on the development of public open spaces in all barangays (Office of the Sangguniang Bayan of Palanan, Isabela, 2023). According to this ordinance, an equivalent of at least 2 percent of each barangay’s total area should be converted to open spaces within one year of approval of the ordinance. Alongside this plan, the municipality must create a green space development committee and prohibit acts such as cutting trees, strongly discouraging it by strictly imposing liabilities and fees on anyone who destroys public open spaces.

Legazpi City, along with other cities in the country such as Angeles City, Ormoc City, Cagayan de Oro City, Tagum City, also developed urban design recommendations for improvement of its open spaces to enhance disaster resilience as part of the UN-HABITAT Philippines’ Building Climate Resiliency through Urban Plans and Designs (BCRUPD) project (Philippines’ Department of Human Settlements and Urban Development, 2023; UNHABITAT, n.d.). This project included workshops with the DHSUD and other local organizations in identifying city adaptation strategies to address DRR and climate challenges. Part of the strategies included the addition of tree cover along streetscapes and parks while also looking into materials that can reflect greenhouse gases (GHG) to eventually reduce heat in the urban areas.

Numerous recommendations are proposed to be translated into regulations that will hopefully influence iterations in the city’s CLUP, Zoning Ordinances, and other related plans and policies.

These local initiatives show the extensive demand to provide planning strategies that can safeguard open spaces among different sectoral categories, of all scales and types.

Aside from the different initiatives of cities and local government offices, open spaces are soon being streamlined into policy as Senate and House bills and resolutions are being drafted for the establishment of the Philippine National Public Parks System (PNPS). These legislative efforts uphold the importance of open spaces at the planning level of the Philippines. A few examples of Senate and House Bills that support the creation of the PNPS are: (1) S.B. 1588, (2) H.B.618, and (3) H.B. 3495.

While there are notable efforts globally on open spaces implementation, there are identified gaps that need to be considered in our country-level policies and guidelines:

- 1. The need to improve the specific guidelines and strategies for the establishment, operations, maintenance, and management of open spaces in accordance with government policies and PPAs is identified.
- 2. There is still a challenge in integrating open spaces in ecological profiling, as well as in urban and environmental planning and development initiatives, given the uniqueness of challenges in different LGUs.
- 3. Minimal specific guidelines also downplay the need for open spaces despite its importance. Although guidelines and ordinances are being created by different cities, those crafting master plans may need further guidance on the implementation of these concepts at the spatial level.
- 4. There is a weakness in applying management tools and guidance on the assessment and monitoring of open spaces PPAs, further challenging local planners on how adjustments can be made to accommodate the everchanging times and climate, and disaster challenges.
- 5. Some of our planning documents lack recommendations on incorporating sustainable design principles and strategies in accordance with the global SDGs.
- 6. There is an absence of guidelines on the equitability and accessibility of open spaces for public use.
- 7. There is limited guidance on the long-term operations, maintenance, and management of open spaces.
- 8. Categorization of open space is diverse globally and will demand a thorough review of a categorization that is apt for our country’s situation.
- 9. A challenge on how open spaces can be operationalized and prepared towards acquiring funding windows that can prioritize its stewardship, creation, and maintenance.


Ultimately, a coordinated approach is necessary for creating successful open space development solutions, with the involvement of the LGUs in partnership with the communities (both human and non-human) being crucial as the beneficiaries of these solutions.

###### FY 2024 LGSF-GREEN GREEN GREEN PROGRAM DESIGN PRINCIPLES

The beneficiary provinces, cities, and municipalities must demonstrate a balance of the design principles—’Designing for the Environment,’ ‘Designing for Resilience,’ and ‘Designing for People’—in their project design to ensure that the project will preserve natural resources, enhance disaster preparedness and employ participatory governance in the development of green infrastructure. Furthermore, the specific design strategies to be adopted will depend on the nature of the project and site characteristics, among other considerations.

- 3. Resilient. Open spaces must be able to withstand, bounce back, adapt to, and recover from both expected and unexpected changes, particularly those associated with disaster risks, climate change, and urbanization. They include considerations of biodiversity and ecosystem services to be able to deliver this aim, with elements such as native vegetation, permeable surfaces, and water management features as important considerations to enhance resilience. Ultimately, the goal of open spaces is not only sustainable and beneficial in the present, but also prepared for the uncertainties of the future.
- 4. Livable. Open spaces will significantly contribute to the quality of life in a community and aid in placemaking. They are designed to be safe, comfortable, and attractive, inclusive, and accessible - encouraging people to spend time in them. They offer a variety of amenities and activities catering to a wide range of interests and age groups. These spaces also promote health and well-being by providing opportunities for physical activity, relaxation, and social interaction. Furthermore, they enhance the aesthetic appeal of the community, contributing to a sense of place and community pride. Ultimately, the goal is to create open spaces that make the community a more enjoyable, healthy, and satisfying place to live.


The principle, “Designing for the Environment” entails schemes, approaches, or design elements considered in the overall project design that respect the existing natural site attributes and preserve, protect, or enhance those attributes in the site development plan.

The principle, “Designing for Resilience” entails schemes, approaches, or design elements considered in the overall project design that: (1) preserve, improve or add green and/or blue infrastructures that go beyond the business-as-usual level and help maintain critical ecosystem functions or address priority climate or disaster risks, (2) ensure that the design solutions can manage climate and disaster risks and opportunities as prescribed under the Urban Planning and Design for Climate Resilience of DHSUD, and (3) integrate project components that minimize generation of waste and promote ease-of-maintenance for long-term sustainability.

These guiding principles as pillars of open space development in the country are aligned with the prescribed design concepts and environmental principles of the FY 2024 LGSF-Green, Green, Green Program (DBM LBC No. 158, dated July 11, 2024, consistent with FY 2024 GAA, RA No. 11975).

To illustrate, by prioritizing sustainability, LGUs can create green open areas that can enhance biodiversity and foster a healthier ecosystem. Responsiveness is achieved as these open spaces serve as critical community hubs, offering venues for recreation, cultural events, and social interaction for all citizens to enjoy, regardless of age, sex, or disabilities. Furthermore, resilience is enhanced with the creation and management of landscapes that mitigate the impacts of disasters and climate change, ensuring that communities can withstand environmental challenges. When properly executed, these principles can create more livable communities.

The principle, “Designing for People” entails schemes, approaches, or design elements considered in the overall project design that: (1) integrate placemaking process in the project planning and design process, (2) ensure inclusive mobility within the project area, location, and its surroundings, and (3) ensure the usability of the public open space project for all users.

###### E. SYNERGIZED CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM

Open spaces are integral components of urban, peri-urban, and rural landscapes. With a variety of uses, hierarchical size, and site-specific purposes, open spaces are expected to be diverse, with terminologies and typologies being varied across the globe. According to Konijnendijk et al. (2013), the terminology and classification of open spaces are influenced by local traditions, governance structures, and urban planning practices, showcasing a rich tapestry of approaches to urban greenery worldwide. Thus, variations are expected to reflect the unique cultural, geographical, and historical contexts of open spaces from different regions as showcased in its diverse nomenclature.

In the Philippines, the definition of “open space” in the context of land use planning is established by two key legal instruments: Batas Pambansa (BP) 220 and Presidential Decree (PD) 1216. BP 220 allocates open space for circulation, community facilities, parks/playgrounds, easements, and courts, whereas PD 1216 provides a broader definition, reserving open space for parks, playgrounds, recreational areas, schools, roads, places of worship, hospitals, health centers, barangay centers, and similar facilities.

This Guidelines adopts and builds on the definition of Open Space, which is “an umbrella term that shall refer to land spaces that is either predominantly undeveloped or set aside in terms of any law, land use policy, zoning or spatial plan, for the purpose of preservation, conservation, regeneration, and creation of more open spaces.” In the context of human settlements planning and management, this definition extends to open spaces and vacant lands that are both publicly and privately owned.

Blue Open Space (BOS) refers to an open space that primarily focuses on waterrelated features such as rivers, lakes, wetlands, and coastal zones.

![image 38](Open Space Development Guidelines_images/imageFile38.png)

Green Open Space (GOS) refers to an open space primarily characterized by its natural vegetation and landforms. These spaces include parks, forests, grasslands, and other types of vegetated landscapes.

Non-Blue/Non-Green Open Space (NOS) refers to an open space that does not primarily feature natural water bodies (blue spaces) or dense vegetation (green spaces). These spaces are characterized by their urban or developed landscapes, which may include urban plazas, industrial zones, transport infrastructure, and paved surfaces. other built environments, and other non-vegetated areas.

### Open Space Development in the CLUP

###### A. OSD 4-Step Process

An overview of the step-by-step implementation of open space development is discussed in this section. This process (Figure 13), involving four main steps or activities, aims to serve as a primary tool for creating more sustainable, responsive, resilient, and livable open spaces. These steps draw on a wealth of research and case studies, both local and international, to offer practical insights and recommendations on open space planning, especially at the local level.

It also emphasizes a participatory approach in implementation, encouraging stakeholders at all levels of the process to engage in the pre-planning, planning, implementation, and sustaining of open spaces. By doing so, it aims to foster a sense of ownership and stewardship among all, thereby ensuring the long-term success and sustainability of open spaces initiatives in the country.

![image 83](Open Space Development Guidelines_images/imageFile83.png)

Figure 13. The 4-Step Process to Open Space Development

The objectives of each step are the following:

- Step 1 – Pre-Plan (Visioning and Goal Setting, Assessment, and Inventory) Establish clear vision and objectives for open spaces and understand the current state and challenges of open spaces.
- Step 2 – Plan (Planning and Design) Create open space development plans and designs that align with the landscape character, vision, and goals from Step 1.
- Step 3 – Implement (Implementation) Develop a clear and actionable implementation roadmap to preserve, conserve, regenerate, and/or create open spaces.
- Step 4 – Sustain (Monitoring, Evaluation, Assessment) Establish mechanisms to track progress, assess status, and ensure the open spaces meet its goals. Also, ensure that strategies to ensure the quality of open space are in place.


The result of following this process can provide the LGU with an overall framework and detailed guide or action plan on planning and implementation of open space policies and projects across different zonal influences.

Ultimately, implementing this process will also contribute to the country’s efforts and solutions to achieve its targets in the Philippine Development Plan towards establishing livable communities, as well as meeting international commitments on UN Sustainable Development Goals on developing sustainable communities, enhancing environmental quality, and accelerating climate action.

###### B. OSD Mainstreaming into the CLUP

Open spaces, parks, and other green spaces are reflected in existing land use policies/guidelines and other national laws and regulations. However, these existing policies are mostly outdated and provide varying definitions for open and green spaces across planning levels and development applications. This Open Space Development Guidelines aim to prescribe a more comprehensive classification of Open Spaces to facilitate its uniform planning and development across LGUs in the context of land use planning.

The DHSUD (HLURB) Comprehensive Land Use Plan Guidebook (Volume 3) defines open space as an area where permanent buildings shall not be allowed, and which may only be used as forest, buffer/greenbelts, parks, and playgrounds.

In terms of zoning, open spaces can be incorporated as part of several base zones such as Parks and Recreation Zone, Buffer/Greenbelt Zone, Forest Zone, National Parks, NIPAS Zone, Tourism Zone, Planned Unit Development Zone, Ecotourism Overlay Zone, Critical Habitat Overlay Zone, and Key Biodiversity Overlay Zone, among others. Moreover, it is important to highlight that green and open spaces can be incorporated as an allowable use in other base zones (as overlay zones) to promote mixed-use development that is efficient, sustainable, and responsive to local needs/conditions.

The OSD 4-Step Process that was discussed in the previous section is the main reference for the mainstreaming of open space development in the Comprehensive Land Use Plan (CLUP) and Zoning Ordinances (ZO) of LGUs that will later on be translated to programs, projects, and activities (PPAs) in the Comprehensive Development Plan (CDP) and Investment Programs of LGUs. How open spaces can be integrated in the said documents through different entry points, strategies, and activities are crucial in ensuring that open spaces become an integral part of the planning process. Mainstreaming OSD into the CLUP/ZO will also highlight the significance of open spaces in the overall land use plan, policies, and major programs of the LGU.

- CLUP STEP 1 Organize an Open Space Development Technical Working Group (TWG) To effectively enhance open spaces within the LGU, the first crucial step is to organize a dedicated Open Space Development Technical Working Group (TWG). This group will be responsible for spearheading initiatives and coordinating efforts related to open space planning and development.

Open Space Development TWG Composition The TWG should comprise representatives from relevant LGU departments, key agencies, and recognized experts in open space development. A recommended composition includes:

- • Local Planning and Development Office: 1 member
- • Local Zoning Office: 1 member
- • Local Environment and Natural Resources Office: 1 member
- • Local Health Office: 1 member
- • Local Tourism Office: 1 member
- • Professional Organizations (e.g., PIEP, PALA, UAP): 1-2 members
- • Academe: 1 member
- • Private Sector: 1-2 members


Defining Roles, Responsibilities, and Outputs

A critical component of establishing the TWG is clearly defining the roles, responsibilities, and expected outputs for all participating departments, offices, agencies, and experts. This clarity will ensure efficient collaboration and accountability. The immediate output of this step will be an Executive Order from the Local Chief Executive. This order will formally establish the Open Space Development TWG and thoroughly outline its defined roles and responsibilities.

Open Space Information Collation

Simultaneously, the TWG will begin the vital task of collating all existing information on open spaces. This includes gathering details on past and present projects, programs, and activities (PPAs) related to open space development within the LGU. This comprehensive overview will serve as a foundational resource for the TWG’s future work.

- CLUP STEP 2 Identify key stakeholders related to open space development Effective open space development hinges on the active involvement and collaboration of diverse stakeholders.


Identify stakeholders who have an interest or play a role in the open space planning/ development process The first phase involves a comprehensive identification of all individuals, groups, or entities that have a vested interest in, or are significantly impacted by, open space planning and development.

This includes, but is not limited to:

- a. Local Government Units (LGUs): Beyond the Open Space Development TWG, this includes other relevant LGU departments and offices (e.g., Engineering, Social Welfare, Culture and the Arts, Agriculture), barangay officials, and local council members
- b. National Government Agencies (NGAs): Agencies with mandates related to land use, environment, health, tourism, and culture (e.g., Department of Human Settlements and Urban Development (DHSUD), Department of Environment and Natural Resources (DENR), Department of Tourism (DOT), Department of Health (DOH), National Commission for Culture and the Arts (NCCA), Department of Public Works and Highways (DPWH)
- c. Community Groups and Residents: Homeowners’ associations, youth organizations, senior citizens’ groups, environmental advocates, cultural groups, and informal settler communities
- d. Professional Organizations: Philippine Institute of Environmental Planners (PIEP), Philippine Association of Landscape Architects (PALA), United Architects of the Philippines (UAP), and other relevant professional bodies
- e. Academe and Research Institutions: Universities, colleges, and research centers with expertise in urban planning, landscape architecture, environmental science, public health, and social sciences


- f. Private Sector: Developers, landowners, businesses operating near or adjacent to open spaces, and corporations interested in corporate social responsibility (CSR) initiatives related to green spaces
- g. Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs) and People’s Organizations (POs): Environmental NGOs, community development organizations, and advocacy groups focused on urban planning and public spaces
- h. Indigenous Cultural Communities/Indigenous Peoples (ICCs/IPs): If applicable, communities whose ancestral domains or cultural practices are linked to specific open spaces
- i. Utilities and Infrastructure Providers: Water, electricity, and telecommunications companies whose services intersect with open space planning


###### Conduct a workshop to identify stakeholders

To facilitate this identification, a stakeholder workshop should be conducted. This workshop will serve as a platform for initial engagement, allowing for open dialogue and collective brainstorming to ensure a comprehensive list of stakeholders is generated. The workshop will also begin to surface their diverse interests, potential roles, and concerns.

Develop and implement an Information and Education Campaign (IEC) plan on open spaces To foster broad public understanding, appreciation, and support for open space development, a well-structured Information and Education Campaign (IEC) Plan must be developed and implemented. This plan should:

- • Define clear objectives. What knowledge, attitudes, or behaviors do we want to influence regarding open spaces? (e.g., increased awareness of benefits, promotion of responsible use, active participation in planning)
- • Identify target audiences. Tailor messages and communication channels to specific groups (e.g., general public, youth, businesses, specific communities)
- • Develop key messages. Craft clear, concise, and compelling messages highlighting the value and benefits of open spaces (e.g., “Open spaces: essential for health and well-being,” “Green spaces for a greener future,” “Your voice matters in shaping our public spaces”)
- • Select appropriate communication channels and materials. Utilize a mix of traditional and modern media, considering the target audience:

♦ Print materials: Brochures, flyers, posters, newsletters, infographics

♦ Digital media: Social media campaigns, dedicated website sections, online videos, webinars

♦ Community engagement: Public forums, barangay assemblies, school programs, community clean-up drives, walking tours of existing open spaces

♦ Local media: Radio advertisements, local TV features, newspaper articles

♦ Events: Public art installations, cultural performances in open spaces, and environmental awareness events

- • Establish a timeline and budget. Allocate resources and set realistic timelines for IEC activities
- • Define monitoring and evaluation mechanisms. How will the effectiveness of the IEC campaign be measured? (e.g., surveys, social media engagement, participation rates in activities)


The IEC plan should emphasize the co-benefits of open spaces, such as improved public health, environmental resilience (e.g., flood control, air quality), economic opportunities (e.g., tourism, local businesses), and enhanced community social cohesion. It should also highlight opportunities for public participation in the planning and design processes, empowering citizens to be active partners in shaping their urban environment.

- CLUP STEP 3 Align the vision with the objectives of open space development A robust open space development strategy must be firmly rooted in the overarching vision and objectives of the Local Government Unit (LGU). This step focuses on ensuring seamless integration with existing plans and establishing a shared understanding of desired outcomes for open spaces.


Review the vision, goals, and objectives of the existing CLUP and align with the objectives of open space development The first crucial task is to conduct a thorough review of the vision, goals, and objectives articulated in the existing Comprehensive Land Use Plan (CLUP). This review will identify:

- • Existing provisions related to open spaces. What does the current CLUP say about parks, greenways, recreational areas, public plazas, and other types of open spaces?


- • Overarching development goals. How do the CLUP’s broader objectives (e.g., economic growth, environmental sustainability, social equity, disaster resilience) intersect with open space development?
- • Identified gaps or areas for enhancement. Are there aspects of open space planning that are not adequately addressed in the current CLUP?


Following this review, the objectives of open space development must be strategically aligned and integrated with the CLUP’s framework. This involves:

- • Identifying synergies. Highlighting how well-planned open spaces contribute to achieving the CLUP’s overall vision and goals (e.g., open spaces as critical infrastructure for flood mitigation, promoting active transport, enhancing tourism, or providing venues for community interaction)
- • Proposing amendments or refinements. If necessary, identifying specific areas within the CLUP where language pertaining to open spaces can be strengthened, expanded, or made more explicit to reflect current best practices and emerging needs. This could involve proposing new zones/subzones, revising density controls to encourage open space provision, or integrating open space considerations into infrastructure development guidelines
- • Ensuring consistency. Verifying that the proposed open space development objectives do not contradict any existing CLUP provisions, but rather complement and reinforce them


###### Agreement on open space-related vision descriptor/s

To ensure a unified direction for all future open space initiatives, it is essential to reach a consensus on specific vision descriptor/s that encapsulate the desired future state of open spaces. This process should involve the Open Space Development TWG and key stakeholders to foster collective ownership.

These vision descriptors should be:

- • Concise and with impact. Easy to understand and remember
- • Aspirational yet achievable. Reflecting an ideal future while remaining realistic
- • Reflective of local context and aspirations. Tailored to the unique characteristics and needs of the LGU
- • Comprehensive. Encompassing the multi-faceted benefits of open spaces (e.g., ecological, social, recreational, cultural, economic)


The agreed-upon vision descriptor/s will serve as a guiding principle for all subsequent planning, design, implementation, and management efforts related to open spaces in Quezon City. - CLUP STEP 4 Identify and profile the existing and potential open spaces (Open Space Inventory) A comprehensive understanding of the LGU’s current and potential open spaces is fundamental to effective planning and development. This step involves creating a detailed Open Space Inventory, serving as a baseline for all subsequent initiatives.


List and profile existing and potential open spaces for development through site survey and validation This process involves a systematic approach to identifying and documenting all open spaces within the LGU, differentiating between existing and potential sites.

- 1. Site Survey and Validation: A multi-faceted approach combining desk research with on-the-ground validation is essential:


###### a. Desk Research

- • Review existing official maps and plans. Utilize the CLUP, zoning maps, cadastral maps, barangay development plans, and any available aerial imagery or GIS data from relevant LGU departments (e.g., City Planning and Development Department, Environmental Protection and Waste Management Department, Parks Development and Administration Department).
- • Consult previous studies and reports. Gather information from any past open space assessments, environmental impact assessments, or urban planning studies conducted in the LGU.


- • Engage with LGU departments. Obtain data and insights from departments directly involved with land management, public works, health, and tourism regarding their assets and ongoing projects related to open spaces.
- • Utilize GeoRiskPH. Review hazards and risk information available in GeoRiskPH, Government’s central data for hazards and risk information sourced from the mandated science agencies.


###### b. Field Surveys and Validation

- • Physical site visits. Conduct systematic ground surveys across all barangays in the LGUs. This will involve visual inspection, taking photographs, and noting key features
- • GPS data collection. Accurately record the precise geographical coordinates and boundaries of each identified open space using GPS devices or mapping applications
- • Stakeholder interviews. Engage with local barangay officials, community leaders, residents, and property owners during field visits to gather anecdotal information, historical context, current uses, and potential challenges or opportunities for each site
- • Verification of potential sites. Beyond existing open spaces, actively seek out and validate potential sites such as:


♦ Underutilized or vacant public lands: Government-owned lots that are currently undeveloped or not serving their intended purpose

♦ Redevelopment areas: Sites designated for urban renewal where open space provision can be integrated

♦ Easements and setbacks: Areas legally designated for public use or environmental protection

♦ Unused private lands with potential for acquisition or public-private partnerships: Lands that could be converted into open spaces through various arrangements

- 2. Profiling Each Open Space For each identified open space, a detailed profile should be created, including but not limited to the following information:


- • Unique Identifier: A specific code or name for easy reference
- • Category of Open Space: Determine whether Blue, Green, Non-Blue/Non-Green
- • Type of Open Space: According to the Synergized Classification System (refer to pages 33 to 45)
- • Name of Open Space: The official or commonly recognized name by which the open space is known
- • Location Details: Barangay, street address, and precise coordinates
- • Area/Size: Total land area in square meters
- • Scale and Zonal Influence: Administrative Scale and Ecological Scale
- • Ownership: Public (LGU, national government), private, or mixed. Specify the managing entity for public lands
- • Current Land Use: e.g., parks & recreation, institutional, agriculture, tourism
- • Surrounding Land Use: Adjacent residential, commercial, industrial, or institutional areas
- • Current Use/Function/Purpose: Recreational (playground, sports field), ecological (greenbelt, sanctuary), civic (plaza, public square), commercial (open market area), undeveloped, etc
- • Key Features/Amenities: Presence of trees, vegetation, water bodies, playgrounds, benches, pathways, lighting, restrooms, sports facilities, art installations, etc.
- • Condition Assessment: Current state of maintenance, safety, and environmental quality (e.g. functional, abandoned, idle)
- • Accessibility: Determine whether open, restricted access, etc.
- • Challenges/Constraints: Issues such as informal settlers, security concerns, encroachment, pollution, lack of maintenance, drainage problems, or ownership disputes
- • Opportunities/Potential: How the space could be improved, developed, or utilized for enhanced public benefit (e.g., potential for new amenities, ecological restoration, community gardens, event spaces)


Identify the available open space classification and typologies in the locality To provide a structured framework for the inventory and future planning, it is crucial to identify and define the various open space classifications and typologies relevant to the LGU’s context. This will involve:

- • Reviewing National and Local Standards. Consult national guidelines (e.g., HLURB/DHSUD planning standards, DENR guidelines for protected areas) and existing local ordinances or plans that define different types of open spaces.


- • Developing a Localized Typology. Based on the existing context of the LGU, and informed by the inventory, define a clear set of open space typologies according to the three main classifications: Blue Open Space, Green Open Space, and Non-Blue/Non-Green Open Space. These typologies should go beyond generic classifications and reflect the specific functions, characteristics, and management approaches relevant to the city. Refer to pages 33 to 45 and pages 86 to 194.


This classification system will aid in analysis, policy formulation, and targeted development interventions, ensuring that diverse open space needs across the LGU are addressed strategically. The inventory will be a living document, regularly updated to reflect changes in land use and new open space initiatives.

- CLUP STEP 6 Integrate open space development strategies into the development thrust Effective open space development is not an isolated endeavor; it must be deeply interwoven with the LGU’s broader development goals and spatial strategies. This step focuses on establishing open spaces as fundamental components of the LGU’s growth and sustainability.


Integrate open space development as a spatial strategy that supports the development thrust of the LGU This involves strategically positioning open space development as a core component of the LGU’s overarching development agenda. It requires:

- • Reviewing LGU’s Current Development Thrusts. Begin by thoroughly understanding the primary development directions and priorities articulated in LGU’s existing Comprehensive Land Use Plan (CLUP), Executive-Legislative Agenda (ELA), Annual Investment Program (AIP), and other relevant city plans and policies. These thrusts might include:

♦ Economic Competitiveness: Attracting investments, fostering innovation, creating job opportunities

♦ Environmental Sustainability and Resilience: Climate change adaptation, disaster risk reduction, biodiversity conservation, pollution control

♦ Social Equity and Inclusivity: Poverty reduction, access to basic services, cultural preservation, gender sensitivity

♦ Good Governance and Urban Mobility: Efficient service delivery, smart city initiatives, integrated transport systems

♦ Tourism and Cultural Heritage: Promoting local attractions, preserving historical sites

- • Identifying Synergies and Interdependencies. For each identified development thrust, articulate how integrated open space planning and development can directly contribute to its achievement. For instance:

♦ Economic Competitiveness: Well-designed open spaces can enhance property values, attract businesses (e.g., cafes, recreation services), support creative industries, and serve as venues for economic activities like farmers’ markets or festivals

♦ Environmental Sustainability and Resilience: Open spaces are critical green infrastructure for flood control (rain gardens, permeable surfaces), urban heat island mitigation (tree canopy), air quality improvement, carbon sequestration, and habitat provision. They enhance the city’s resilience to climate change impacts

♦ Social Equity and Inclusivity: Accessible open spaces provide equitable recreational opportunities for all residents, foster community interaction, support mental and physical health, and offer safe public gathering places

♦ Urban Mobility: Linear parks and greenways can serve as pedestrian and cycling networks, promoting active transport and reducing reliance on motorized vehicles

♦ Tourism and Cultural Heritage: Iconic parks, plazas, and historical open spaces can be significant tourist attractions, enhancing the city’s image and cultural appeal

- • Developing Policy Recommendations. Based on the identified synergies, formulate concrete policy recommendations for integrating open space considerations across all relevant LGU departments and programs. This could include:


♦ Mandating open space provision in new developments

♦ Integrating green infrastructure standards into building codes

♦ Prioritizing investment in open spaces within high-growth areas

♦ Establishing inter-departmental coordinating mechanisms for open space initiatives

###### Evaluate and select the most suitable development and spatial strategy

This step involves analyzing various approaches to open space development and selecting the one that best aligns with the LGU’s vision, goals, and unique characteristics. This is often an iterative process informed by the Open Space Inventory and stakeholder feedback.

- • Analyze Different Spatial Strategies. Consider various models for how open spaces can be integrated into the urban fabric, like:


♦ Network Strategy: Developing interconnected green corridors, linear parks, and pedestrian/cyclist paths that link existing open spaces and different districts

♦ Hub-and-Spoke Strategy: Focusing on creating significant central open spaces (hubs) that are connected to smaller neighborhood open spaces (spokes)

♦ Distributed Open Space System: Ensuring equitable distribution of smaller, accessible open spaces across all barangays

♦ Eco-corridor Strategy: Prioritizing open spaces that enhance ecological connectivity and biodiversity

♦ Water-sensitive Urban Design (WSUD): Integrating open spaces as critical components of stormwater management and flood resilience

♦ Transit-Oriented Development (TOD) with Green Spaces: Integrating open spaces around transport hubs to enhance walkability and livability

- • Conduct Feasibility and Impact Assessment. For each potential strategy, evaluate its:

♦ Feasibility: Is it technically, financially, and legally viable for the LGU?

♦ Impact: How effectively will it achieve the established open space goals and objectives? What are the potential social, environmental, and economic benefits and challenges?

♦ Scalability: Can the strategy be implemented incrementally and expanded over time?

♦ Resilience: Does it contribute to the city’s climate change adaptation and disaster risk reduction efforts?

- • Stakeholder Consultation and Consensus Building. Present the evaluated strategies to the Open Space Development TWG and key stakeholders for discussion and feedback. Facilitate a collaborative decision-making process to select the most suitable strategy that garners broad support and best addresses the LGU’s needs and aspirations


###### Preparing a Structure/ Concept Plan with emphasis on open spaces

The culmination of the above steps is the creation of a Structure/Concept Plan that visually and conceptually articulates the LGU’s existing and proposed open space system. This plan serves as a high-level blueprint for future development, prioritizing open spaces as integral urban components.

- • Visual Representation of the Selected Strategy. The plan should graphically depict the chosen spatial strategy (e.g., network, distributed, eco-corridor)
- • Identification of Key Open Space Systems:

♦ Major Parks and Green Hubs: Illustrate the location and role of existing large parks and proposed new central open spaces

♦ Green Corridors and Linkages: Map out proposed greenways, linear parks, river/creek easements, and pedestrian/cyclist paths that connect different areas and open spaces

♦ Neighborhood Green Spaces: Indicate areas for the development or enhancement of smaller, local parks and plazas

♦ Ecological Preservation Areas: Delineate areas primarily for biodiversity conservation and environmental protection (e.g., urban forests, wetlands)

♦ Cultural and Heritage Open Spaces: Highlight important public plazas, historical gardens, and civic spaces

- • Conceptual Design Principles. Outline the design philosophy for future open space development, including principles related to:

♦ Accessibility and Inclusivity: Ensuring universal access for all users

♦ Sustainability: Use of native plants, water conservation, and sustainable materials

♦ Multi-functionality: Designing spaces that serve multiple purposes (recreation, ecological, cultural) ♦ Identity and Placemaking: Creating unique and memorable places that reflect the LGU’s character ♦ Safety and Security: Designing for passive surveillance and community ownership

- • Integration with Other Urban Systems. The plan should conceptually illustrate how the open space system integrates with:

♦ Transportation Networks: Pedestrian paths, bike lanes

♦ Water Management Systems: Stormwater management features, green infrastructure

♦ Land Use Zones: How open spaces relate to residential, commercial, and institutional areas

♦ Future Growth Areas: Identification of where new open spaces will be critical for planned urban expansion

- • Phasing and Prioritization (Conceptual). Provide an initial conceptual indication of potential phasing for major open space projects, acknowledging that detailed phasing will be developed in later stages.


This Structure/Concept Plan will be a vital tool for communicating the open space vision to stakeholders, guiding more detailed planning efforts, and serving as a basis for securing necessary resources and approvals.

- CLUP STEP 7 Prepare Open Space Maps based on the Open Space Inventory Building upon the established vision, goals, and the comprehensive inventory, this phase focuses on translating strategic directions into detailed spatial plans, governing policies, actionable programs, and a robust investment framework for the LGU’s open spaces.


Determine land requirements and supply This step bridges the gap between the identified open space needs and the available resources.

- • Analyze Demand

♦ Population-based standards: May refer to international benchmarks (e.g., UN-Habitat suggests that cities aim for 15-20 m² per person of public green space), or determine the ideal open space per person for LGUs’ current and projected population

♦ Accessibility standards: Assess the spatial distribution of existing open spaces against established accessibility targets (e.g., X% of residents living within a Y-minute walk of an open space)

♦ Functional needs: Evaluate the demand for specific types of open spaces (e.g., playgrounds for children, sports fields for youth, quiet areas for seniors, community gardens, civic plazas) based on demographic analysis and stakeholder feedback

♦ Environmental needs: Quantify the land required for critical ecological functions (e.g., stormwater management, urban forestry, biodiversity corridors) to meet climate resilience and sustainability goals

- • Assess Supply

♦ Utilize the Open Space Inventory: Based on the detailed inventory, quantify the total area and specific typologies of existing open spaces

♦ Identify Potential Supply: From the inventory, highlight and quantify areas identified as potential open spaces (e.g., vacant public lands, underutilized private lots, areas suitable for linear parks, lands for potential acquisition)

♦ Analyze Land Tenure and Availability: Determine the ownership status (public, private), any encumbrances, and the feasibility of acquiring or utilizing identified potential sites

- • Identify Gaps and Opportunities. Compare the determined land requirements (demand) with the available and potential supply. This analysis will clearly show:


♦ Deficient areas: Barangays or districts with insufficient open space provision

♦ Specific typologies needed: Shortfalls in playgrounds, sports facilities, ecological areas, etc.

♦ Strategic opportunities: Identification of large tracts of land or interconnected areas with high potential for open space development

Prepare Open Space Maps based on the Open Space Inventory Based on the Open Space Inventory and the land requirement/supply analysis, a series of detailed, georeferenced maps will be prepared. These maps will serve as visual guides for planning and implementation.

- • Existing Open Space Map A comprehensive map showcasing all currently identified open spaces, categorized (Blue, Green, Non-Blue/NonGreen) and by their established typologies (e.g., wetlands, community parks, plazas). Include key features and condition.
- • Proposed Open Space Development Map This map will be the core spatial strategy, illustrating:


♦ New Open Space Locations: Clearly delineate the proposed sites for new open spaces identified through the land requirement analysis

♦ Open Space Enhancement/Upgrading Areas: Highlight existing open spaces slated for significant improvements, renovations, or expansion

♦ Green Corridors and Linkages: Map out proposed linear parks, pedestrian/bike networks, and ecological corridors that connect different open spaces and urban areas

♦ Integration with Future Development: Show how proposed open spaces are strategically integrated with planned urban growth areas, transportation networks, and critical infrastructure

♦ Priority Zones: Indicate areas where open space interventions are most critical based on identified deficiencies, population density, or environmental vulnerability

• Thematic Open Space Maps (as needed)

♦ Accessibility Map: Illustrates areas within a defined walking/cycling distance of open spaces

♦ Ecological Connectivity Map: Highlights green infrastructure networks, biodiversity hotspots, and riparian zones

♦ Vulnerability Map: Shows open spaces’ role in flood control, heat island mitigation, and disaster risk reduction

These maps will utilize GIS (Geographic Information System) technology for accuracy, ease of updating, and spatial analysis.

###### Formulate the policies that will govern the specific classification and typologies of open spaces

To ensure the sustainable management and development of open spaces, a robust policy framework is essential. These policies will govern the acquisition, development, management, and protection of different open space classifications and typologies.

- • Land Use and Zoning Policies

♦ Mandatory Open Space Provisions: Policies requiring a minimum percentage of open space in new residential, commercial, and mixed-use developments

♦ Incentives for Open Space Provision: Offering density bonuses, tax incentives, or streamlined permitting for developers who exceed minimum open space requirements or incorporate innovative green infrastructure ♦ Protection of Existing Open Spaces: Policies to prevent the conversion or encroachment of designated

public open spaces

♦ Open Space Overlay Zones: Special zoning regulations for critical green corridors, ecological areas, or waterfronts

- • Design and Development Standards

♦ Accessibility Guidelines: Policies ensuring universal design principles are applied to all new and renovated open spaces

♦ Green Infrastructure Standards: Requirements for integrating sustainable urban drainage systems, native landscaping, and permeable surfaces

♦ Maintenance and Management Standards: Guidelines for routine maintenance, waste management, security, and horticultural practices for different open space typologies

♦ Safety and Security Protocols: Policies related to lighting, emergency access, and public surveillance in open spaces

♦ Acquisition and Land Banking Policies. Strategies for the acquisition of private lands for public open space, including purchase, easement, land banking, and mechanisms for public-private partnerships

- • Community Engagement and Co-Management Policies. Guidelines for involving local communities in the planning, design, and management of neighborhood-level open spaces
- • Enforcement and Monitoring. Policies outlining mechanisms for enforcing open space regulations and monitoring compliance


These policies will be integrated into the CLUP and relevant local ordinances, ensuring their legal enforceability.

Conceptualize PPAs for implementation (including engagements/ partnerships with private, local, and international investors) Based on the open space goals, objectives, and the spatial strategy, concrete PPAs will be conceptualized. These PPAs will translate the policies and plans into tangible actions.

- • Pilot Projects: Initiate small-scale, high-impact projects in key areas to demonstrate benefits and build public support
- • Park Development/Redevelopment Programs: Specific projects for establishing new parks or comprehensively upgrading existing ones, including design, construction, and landscaping
- • Green Corridor/Linear Park Initiatives: PPAs focused on developing interconnected pedestrian and cycling paths, green infrastructure along rivers/canals, or converting underutilized spaces into linear parks


- • Community Garden Programs: Support for the establishment and maintenance of urban agriculture initiatives in designated open spaces
- • Ecological Restoration Projects: PPAs aimed at rehabilitating degraded natural areas within open spaces (e.g., wetland restoration, urban forest regeneration)
- • Public Art and Cultural Programs: Integration of public art installations, performance spaces, and cultural events within open spaces
- • IEC and Capacity Building Programs: Ongoing information and education campaigns, as well as training programs for LGU staff and community members on open space management
- • Technology Integration: PPAs for smart park management systems, real-time monitoring of environmental parameters, and digital citizen engagement platforms
- • Engagements/Partnerships with Private, Local, and International Investors: Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs): Explore models like build-operate-transfer (BOT), design-build-operate (DBO), or joint ventures for large-scale open space development or management
- • Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) Initiatives: Engage corporations to fund or implement specific open space projects as part of their CSR programs
- • Land Donations/Easements: Encourage private landowners to donate land for public open space or establish conservation easements
- • Academe/Research Partnerships: Collaborate with universities for research, design expertise, and monitoring
- • International Grants and Funding: Pursue funding opportunities from international development agencies, environmental organizations, and philanthropic foundations
- • Community Co-management Agreements: Formalize agreements with local communities for shared responsibility in open space management


Budgetary and investment planning of open space PPAs This step involves developing a realistic and sustainable financial framework for the implementation of the open space development plan.

- • Cost Estimation. Develop detailed cost estimates for each PPA, including:

♦ Land acquisition costs (if applicable)

♦ Design and planning fees

♦ Construction and infrastructure development costs

♦ Landscaping and planting costs

♦ Ongoing maintenance and operational costs

♦ Program management and monitoring costs

- • Funding Sources Identification. Explore a diverse range of funding sources:

♦ LGU Annual Budget: Allocation from the LGU’s general fund

♦ Special Local Funds: E.g., Environment and Natural Resources Fund, Tourism Development Fund, Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Fund

♦ Development Fees/Exactions: Fees collected from developers for open space provision or in-lieu fees

♦ Bonds/Loans: Issuing municipal bonds or securing loans from financial institutions for major projects

♦ National Government Allocations: Lobbying for funds from national agencies (e.g., DBM, DENR, DOH, DPWH, DOT)

♦ Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs): Attracting private capital for specific projects

♦ Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR): Securing direct funding or in-kind support from corporations

♦ Grants and Philanthropy: Applying for grants from local, national, and international foundations or organizations

♦ User Fees/Concessions: Generating revenue from certain open space amenities or activities (e.g., sports facility rentals, event permits, kiosks)

♦ Crowdfunding/Community Fundraising: For smaller, community-led projects

- • Investment Prioritization. Based on the identified gaps, strategic importance, feasibility, and potential impact, prioritize PPAs for funding and implementation. This may involve a multi-year investment program.
- • Monitoring and Evaluation Framework. Establish mechanisms to track expenditures, assess the financial performance of PPAs, and evaluate the return on investment for open space development.


###### CLUP STEP 8 Create overlay zones to identify allowable uses and activities for each open space type

To ensure the effective management, protection, and enhancement of the LGU’s open spaces, it is crucial to establish clear regulatory frameworks and robust legislative measures. This involves creating specific overlay zones and crafting allowable use and activities for each open space type.

Create overlay zones to identify allowable uses and activities for each open space classification and type

Open space overlay zones are a powerful planning tool that allows for specific regulations to be applied to designated open spaces, supplementing the underlying base zoning. This ensures that the unique character and function of each open space type are preserved and enhanced.

- • Define Overlay Zone Boundaries Based on the detailed Open Space Maps and the Open Space Inventory, delineate distinct overlay zone boundaries for each significant open space or interconnected open space system. These boundaries should align with the established open space classifications and typologies (e.g., Blue Open Spaces Overlay, Green Open Spaces Overlay, NonBlue/Non-Green Open Spaces Overlay)
- • Specify Allowable Uses and Activities for Each Overlay Zone For each defined open space overlay zone, clearly articulate a list of permitted, conditional, and prohibited uses and activities. This moves beyond general zoning and provides specific guidance for each open space type.

♦ Permitted Uses: Activities that are encouraged and require minimal review (e.g., passive recreation, walking, picnicking in a park; biodiversity conservation in an ecological zone)

♦ Conditional Uses: Activities that may be allowed with specific conditions, permits, or design review to ensure compatibility with the open space’s primary function and character (e.g., temporary events, small-scale concessions, educational programs, public art installations)

♦ Prohibited Uses: Activities that are strictly forbidden due to their negative impact on the open space’s integrity, public enjoyment, or environmental value (e.g., permanent structures not related to park management, heavy vehicle parking, unauthorized vending, dumping of waste, activities causing significant noise or pollution)

- • Integrate Design Guidelines within Overlay Zones Beyond uses, incorporate specific design guidelines for any development or intervention within the overlay zone, ensuring consistency with the open space’s character and the city’s open space vision. This could include guidelines on building height, materials, landscaping, lighting, and signage
- • Establish Management Protocols Outline general management expectations within each overlay zone, such as maintenance responsibilities, waste management, and security considerations

These overlay zones will be formally incorporated into the LGU’s Zoning Ordinance, providing a legal basis for regulating development and activities within designated open spaces.

Craft open space enforcement guidelines through identification of allowable uses and activities for each open space classification and type, as policies for maintaining and protecting designated open spaces

Effective enforcement is paramount to maintaining and protecting designated open spaces. Comprehensive guidelines are needed to ensure consistent application of the established policies and regulations.

- • Based on Allowable Uses and Activities. The enforcement guidelines will directly stem from the detailed list of allowable uses and activities for each open space classification and type, as defined in the overlay zones
- • Identify Responsible Agencies/Departments. Clearly designate which LGU departments, offices, or attached agencies (e.g., City Parks Development and Administration Department, Environmental Protection and Waste Management Department, Department of Public Order and Safety, Barangay officials) are responsible for enforcing specific regulations within each open space type


- • Define Enforcement Procedures Outline the step-by-step process for addressing violations, including:

♦ Monitoring and Surveillance: Regular patrols, community reporting mechanisms, and potential use of technology (CCTV)

♦ Violation Identification and Documentation: Protocols for documenting offenses (e.g., photo evidence, incident reports)

♦ Issuance of Warnings/Notices: Procedures for notifying offenders

♦ Imposition of Penalties/Fines: Clearly defined penalty structures for various violations, as stipulated in relevant ordinances

♦ Abatement and Remediation: Steps for removing unauthorized structures, cleaning up waste, or restoring damaged areas

♦ Legal Action: Procedures for pursuing legal recourse for persistent or severe violations

- • Develop Public Awareness Campaigns on Guidelines Implement an information campaign to educate residents, users, and businesses about the allowable uses, prohibited activities, and enforcement guidelines for open spaces, fostering self-compliance.
- • Training and Capacity Building Provide regular training for enforcement personnel on relevant ordinances, regulations, and best practices for conflict resolution in public spaces.

- CLUP STEP 9 Engage the public and key stakeholders by consulting the contents of the OSDP To effectively consult on the contents of the OSDP, a multi-pronged approach involving various platforms and target groups is essential.

Engage the public and key stakeholders by consulting the contents of the Open Space Development Plan through meetings and public hearings

• Conduct Targeted Consultative Meetings Organize smaller, focused consultative meetings with key stakeholder groups to allow for in-depth discussions and feedback on specific aspects of the OSDP. These meetings should be tailored to the interests and expertise of each group:

♦ Open Space Development Technical Working Group (TWG): Regular, dedicated sessions with the TWG are essential to review drafts, discuss technical details, and refine proposals

♦ Barangay Officials and Residents’ Associations: Conduct meetings at the barangay level, particularly in areas identified for significant open space development or enhancement. This ensures localized input and addresses specific community concerns

♦ Professional Organizations (e.g., PIEP, PALA, UAP): Engage urban planners, landscape architects, and architects to solicit expert technical feedback on design principles, sustainability, and feasibility

♦ Environmental Groups and Advocates: Consult with local environmental NGOs and advocacy groups to ensure the OSDP aligns with ecological principles, biodiversity conservation, and climate resilience efforts

♦ Business Sector and Developers: Engage with real estate developers, business associations, and potential investors to discuss the economic implications of the OSDP, explore partnership opportunities, and address any concerns regarding development regulations

♦ Academe and Research Institutions: Seek input from universities and research bodies for academic insights, best practices, and innovative solutions related to open space planning and management

♦ Vulnerable and Underrepresented Groups: Ensure specific outreach and consultation with marginalized communities, persons with disabilities, senior citizens, and youth to address their unique needs and ensure equitable access to open spaces

These meetings should utilize clear visual aids (maps, conceptual designs, infographics) and provide accessible summaries of the OSDP’s key components.

LGU-wide Public Hearings

Beyond targeted meetings, conduct well-advertised LGU-wide public hearings to gather broader public input on the OSDP. These hearings serve as a formal platform for residents and interested parties to voice their opinions, concerns, and suggestions.

- CLUP STEP 10 Review of the Open Space Policies and adopt the OSDP The culmination of the planning process involves a rigorous review of all formulated open space policies and the formal adoption of the Open Space Development Plan (OSDP). This ensures the plan is legally compliant, strategically sound, and ready for implementation.


Policy/Regulatory compliance checking for identified open spaces in the Open Space Development Plan Before formal submission, a meticulous policy and regulatory compliance check is essential for all identified open spaces and the provisions within the OSDP. This involves:

- • Alignment with Existing National Laws and Regulations Verify that all aspects of the OSDP, including proposed classifications, uses, and development standards, are in full compliance with relevant national laws. This includes, but is not limited to, laws related to:


♦ Environmental Protection: Such as the Philippine Clean Air Act, Clean Water Act, and Ecological Solid Waste Management Act

♦ Urban Development and Housing: Regulations from the Department of Human Settlements and Urban Development (DHSUD), including minimum open space requirements for subdivisions and developments

♦ Disaster Risk Reduction and Management: Provisions for open spaces as evacuation areas or for flood mitigation

♦ Cultural Heritage: Laws protecting historical sites and cultural landscapes that may include open spaces

♦ Accessibility: Adherence to the Batas Pambansa Blg. 344 (Accessibility Law) for universal design in public spaces

- • Consistency with Existing Local Ordinances and Plans Cross-reference the OSDP with current ordinances, particularly the existing Comprehensive Land Use Plan (CLUP) and Zoning Ordinance (ZO).

♦ Identify inconsistencies: Pinpoint any conflicts between the OSDP’s proposals (e.g., new open space classifications, allowable uses in overlay zones, design standards) and existing local laws

♦ Propose necessary amendments: Clearly outline the specific sections of the CLUP, ZO, or other relevant local ordinances that need to be amended or updated to accommodate and enable the OSDP. This includes formally integrating the Open Space Overlay Zones and the detailed allowable uses and activities for each open space type

- • Review of Enforcement Guidelines: Ensure the crafted enforcement guidelines are legally sound, practical to implement, and clearly define responsibilities and penalties
- • Stakeholder Validation: Briefly revisit key legal or technical stakeholders (e.g., City Legal Department, City Engineering Department, relevant national government agency regional offices) to confirm their concurrence on compliance aspects


This thorough review minimizes potential legal challenges and ensures a smooth approval process.

Submit the final draft of the CLUP/ZO for review and approval Once the compliance review is complete and all necessary refinements have been integrated, the final draft of the Open Space Development Plan (OSDP) is ready.

• Formal Endorsement by the Open Space Development TWG The TWG, having spearheaded the plan’s development, formally endorses the final OSDP for submission, signifying their collective approval of its contents and recommendations. The OSDP, along with any proposed amendments to the CLUP/ZO or new open space ordinances, will be submitted to the appropriate local legislative bodies for review and approval.

- CLUP STEP 11 Implement the OSDP With the OSDP formally adopted, the focus shifts to effective implementation. This phase requires strengthening local institutions, engaging the public, and fostering collaborative partnerships.


Strengthen and build local institutional mechanisms on open space development and management Successful OSDP implementation hinges on robust institutional capacity within the LGU. This involves:

• Establishing a Dedicated Open Space Management Office/Division If one doesn’t already exist, create a specialized unit within the LGU (ideally within the City Planning and Development Department or a similar department) with the mandate and resources to oversee OSDP implementation. This office will be responsible for:

♦ Coordinating all open space-related activities across different LGU departments

♦ Managing the Open Space Inventory and GIS database

♦ Monitoring compliance with open space regulations and overlay zones

♦ Developing and managing open space maintenance contracts

♦ Leading public engagement and education initiatives

♦ Seeking external funding and partnerships for open space projects

♦ Reporting on OSDP implementation progress

- • Creating an Open Space Advisory Council/Committee Establish a multi-stakeholder advisory body to provide ongoing guidance and support for OSDP implementation. This council should include representatives from:

♦ Relevant LGU departments (e.g., City Planning, Environment, Parks, Engineering)

♦ Barangay representatives

♦ Professional organizations (e.g., PIEP, PALA, UAP)

♦ Environmental NGOs

♦ Community groups ♦ The business sector

- • Capacity Building and Training Provide ongoing training for LGU staff involved in open space planning, design, management, and enforcement. This includes training on:

♦ Sustainable urban design principles

♦ Green infrastructure technologies

♦ Community engagement techniques

♦ GIS and data management

♦ Enforcement of open space regulations

- • Developing a Long-Term Financial Sustainability Plan Secure dedicated and sustainable funding streams for open space development and maintenance. This may involve:


♦ Dedicated allocations within the LGU’s annual budget

♦ Establishment of a special Open Space Fund

♦ Seeking external grants and partnerships

♦ Exploring revenue-generating opportunities within open spaces (e.g., concessions, event permits)

Inform and educate the public and key stakeholders Sustained public support and participation are crucial for OSDP success. This requires an ongoing information and education campaign (IEC).

- • Develop a Comprehensive Communication Strategy Create a multi-faceted communication strategy to raise awareness about the OSDP, its benefits, and opportunities for public involvement. This should include:

♦ A dedicated section on the LGU’s website with information about the OSDP, maps, and upcoming projects

♦ Active use of social media to share updates, success stories, and engage with the public

♦ Regular press releases and media features highlighting open space initiatives

♦ Public forums, workshops, and community events to explain the OSDP and solicit feedback

♦ Educational materials (brochures, posters, videos) about the value of open spaces and how to use them responsibly

- • Targeted Outreach Programs Design specific outreach programs for different stakeholder groups:

♦ Schools: Integrate open space education into the curriculum and organize field trips to local parks

♦ Businesses: Encourage corporate social responsibility (CSR) initiatives related to open spaces

♦ Community Groups: Partner with barangays and residents’ associations to promote local open space projects

♦ Promote Community Stewardship

♦ Encourage community involvement in the management and maintenance of local open spaces through volunteer programs, “Friends of the Park” groups, and community gardening initiatives

- • Celebrate Successes Publicly recognize and celebrate successful open space projects and community initiatives to build momentum and enthusiasm.


- Establish inter-LGU partnership and/or co-management agreements for open space development Many open space initiatives may benefit from collaboration with neighboring LGUs or co-management agreements with other entities.
- • Identify Opportunities for Inter-LGU Collaboration Explore opportunities for partnerships with neighboring cities or municipalities for:

♦ Developing regional green corridors or ecological networks

♦ Sharing expertise and resources for open space management

♦ Coordinating tourism promotion related to open spaces

♦ Addressing transboundary environmental issues (e.g., watershed management).

- • Formalize Co-Management Agreements Establish formal agreements with other entities for the joint management or development of specific open spaces. Potential partners include:

♦ National government agencies (e.g., DENR, DPWH) for large-scale parks or ecological areas

♦ Private sector companies for public-private partnerships

♦ Universities or research institutions for technical expertise or monitoring

♦ Community organizations for local park management

- • Seek External Funding for Collaborative Projects Jointly apply for grants or funding opportunities from national or international organizations to support inter-LGU or comanaged open space initiatives.


By strengthening local institutions, engaging the public, and fostering collaborative partnerships, LGUs can effectively implement their Open Space Development Plan, creating a greener, healthier, and more livable city for all its residents.

- CLUP STEP 12 Establish monitoring and evaluation mechanisms to track the progress of OSD Effective implementation of an LGU’s Open Space Development Plan (OSDP) requires continuous monitoring and systematic evaluation to ensure progress towards its goals, identify challenges, and facilitate adaptive management. This step outlines the establishment of comprehensive M&E mechanisms.


Establish monitoring and evaluation mechanisms to track the progress of open space development To effectively track progress, clear and measurable Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) must be defined for each objective outlined in the OSDP. These KPIs will provide concrete metrics for assessing success.

Examples of KPIs that may be adopted include:

- • Quantitative Metrics


♦ Area of Accessible Open Space: m 2 or hectares of new or upgraded open space developed/redeveloped per year/per barangay/per 1,000 residents

♦ Tree Canopy Cover: Percentage increase in tree canopy cover within designated open spaces and across the city

♦ Accessibility Proximity: Percentage of residents living within a defined walking distance (e.g., 10-minute walk, 500 meters) of a public open space

♦ Biodiversity Indices: Changes in species richness or abundance in ecological open spaces

♦ Stormwater Capture Capacity: Volume of stormwater managed through green infrastructure in open spaces (m 3 )

♦ Budget Utilization Rate: Percentage of allocated budget utilized for OSDP projects

♦ Number of Community Programs/Events: Quantity of health, cultural, or environmental programs conducted in open spaces

♦ Number of Partnerships: Count of new private, academic, NGO, or inter-LGU partnerships established for open space development/management

♦ Visitor/User Count: Estimated number of users/visitors to key open spaces

♦ Number of Enforcement Actions: Count of violations identified and acted upon/addressed

- • Qualitative Metrics


♦ User Satisfaction: Levels of satisfaction with open space quality, safety, and amenities (measured through surveys, feedback forms)

♦ Perceived Sense of Community: Qualitative assessment of increased social interaction and community cohesion in open spaces

♦ Environmental Quality Perception: Public perception of improved air quality, reduced noise, or enhanced aesthetics

♦ Accessibility Perception: Public perception of ease of access and inclusivity for all user groups

###### Establish data collection systems

Reliable data is the foundation of effective M&E. This involves setting up systematic data collection processes. Baseline Data Collection prior to OSDP implementation ensures comprehensive baseline data for all KPIs that is collected from the Open Space Inventory and other relevant sources. This provides a starting point for measuring change and will be the baseline.

Consequently, this mainstreaming process facilitates the development of a comprehensive Open Space Development Plan (OSDP), the details of which are presented in the next section.

###### C. The Open Space Development Plan (OSDP)

The Open Space Development Plan (OSDP) serves as a strategic roadmap for preserving, conserving, regenerating, and creating open spaces within the jurisdiction of the local government unit (LGU). It is developed as a result of mainstreaming open space development into the Comprehensive Land Use Plan (CLUP) or as a distinct, focused and standalone plan when the OSD 4-Step Process is followed. Its primary purpose is to identify the open space potentials of the LGU and put forth sustainable and inclusive development approaches and PPAs that can address the unique environmental, social, and economic challenges that affect the LGU’s open spaces.

As a key input to the process, any open space profiling can initiate the discovery of unique landscape attributes: physical, biological, socio-economic, cultural, and built environments, highlighting how these elements support an LGU’s vision for development.

The OSDP is also designed to be integrated with other local development plans. This integration ensures that open space considerations are woven into all aspects of local planning and decision-making, and is provided with the necessary resources for implementation.

Coming from the perspective of mainstreaming, an Open Space Development Plan should contain the following:

- I. Brief Profile of the City/Municipality
- II. CLUP Vision, Goals, and Objectives
- III. Classification of Open Spaces in the LGU (Open Space Inventory)
- IV. Development Approaches and Strategies
- V. Open Space Map
- VI. Open Space Policies
- VII. Development Programs for Open Spaces (PPAs)
- VIII. Implementation Strategy, and Monitoring & Evaluation

## Glossary

Open Spaces (OS) are areas in the city or surrounding region that are free of buildings and more open for use. They can be either green open spaces (open spaces) or non-green open spaces (Nopen spaces) (Werner, 2014).

The Comprehensive Land Use Plan Guidebook (Volume 3) defines open space as an area where permanent buildings shall not be allowed, and which may only be used as forest, buffer/greenbelts, parks, and playgrounds (Philippine Housing and Land Use Regulatory Board, 2014).

According to Batas Pambansa (B.P.) 220, “Open space shall refer to areas allocated for the following purposes: a. Circulation, b. Community facilities c. Park/playground d. Easements e. Courts.“

Presidential Decree 1216 also stipulates that “open space shall include schools, roads, places of worship, hospitals, health centers, barangay centers means an area reserved exclusively for parks, playgrounds, recreational and other similar facilities and amenities.”

The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) defines open space as “Any land that is either set aside in terms of any law, zoning scheme or spatial plan, for the purpose of public recreation, conservation, the installation of public infrastructure or agriculture, or predominantly undeveloped and open and has not yet been set aside for a particular purpose in terms of any law, zoning scheme or spatial plan.”

In Section 803 of the IRR of the National Building Code, Open Space at the individual lot level is called Total Open Spaces within Lot or TOSL. Total Open Spaces within Lot (TOSL) are courts, yards, gardens, light wells, uncovered driveways, access roads, and parking spaces consisting of paved or tiled and unpaved areas.

Non-green/Non-blue open spaces (NOS) consist of paved areas within urban areas (Werner, 2014).

Blue Open Space (BOS) or blue infrastructure comprises areas dominated by surface waterbodies or watercourses. In conjunction with green spaces (parks, gardens, etc., specifically: urban open space), it may help in reducing the risks of heat-related illness from high urban temperatures (urban heat island). (Gunawardena et al, 2017).

Green Open Spaces (GOS) are essential for the well-being of urban dwellers. These spaces not only enhance the beauty of the surroundings but also provide important environmental benefits such as reducing carbon emissions and urban heat, improving water quality, attenuates noise, and acting as windbreakers while creating habitats for wildlife. These possible functions resulted in the mandate of a minimum of 9 square meters per person, and ideally 10 to 15 square meters per person of open spaces to be maintained in urban areas (Werner, 2014).

Private open spaces (PRopen spaces) are owned by individuals or institutions and have limited access. They could be gardens, courtyards, or privately planted areas (Werner, 2014).

Public open spaces (PUopen spaces) can be parks, streets, and riverbank greenery owned and maintained by local authorities for the benefit of the community (Werner, 2014). Sometimes called urban open spaces (uos) are publicly accessible green and open spaces and would therefore exclude private backyards, gardens and balconies. PUopen spaces include communal space around apartment buildings, cemeteries, rock walls, street verges and medians, school grounds, rooftop parks, stormwater channels, and surplus parking lots. It may also include open-air, publicly accessible shopping malls that provide opportunities for passive recreation (Byrne and Sipe, 2010).

Also called green public spaces (Sahakian et al., 2020), PUopen spaces are considered outdoor spaces that accommodate biodiversity and are publicly accessible. Drawing on the work of Habermas (1989 and Urry (1995), the concept of the ‘public’ is seen as a place where individuals come together, while ‘space’ is viewed as a reflection of society. Green public spaces can be privately owned and managed, with ‘green’ referring to the natural aspects of the space. Previous research has explored public spaces concerning biodiversity and social inclusion, but there is a lack of literature that considers the environmental and social aspects of sustainability together.

PUopen spaces are carefully planned and managed with the well-being and enjoyment of current and future generations in mind. It plays a vital role in enhancing the quality of life for urban residents and supporting their well-being. PUopen spaces is a crucial element in promoting health and happiness throughout one’s lifetime, and contributes to the overall livability of a community. These spaces allow for interactions with nature, provide habitats for plants and animals, and help define the unique character of a neighborhood. Connecting these areas in a green network will offer numerous benefits for the public, the environment, and biodiversity.

The catchment hierarchy of a PUopen spaces refers to the typical size and the distance a user may be willing to travel to visit the site. This hierarchy reflects the range of distances that people are eager to travel to access open spaces, as well as the sphere of influence and origins of users. Factors that determine the catchment hierarchy include the geographical area being served, the size of the site, the level of use, and its significance (Nochian, et al., 2015).

Public Open Space, which is synonymous with PUopen spaces in this context, is an umbrella term that shall refer to land spaces provided by the national or local government units, designated as parks and intended for physical activity, leisure, and recreational use by the public. It is categorized according to surface or setting and by activity.

Green Networks are the networks of open space, woodlands, wildlife habitat, parks, and other natural areas that sustain clean air, water, and natural resources and enrich the citizens’ quality of life. Sometimes the components of a green network are referred to as green infrastructure and can include paths, woods and open areas (Aberdeen City Council, n.d.).

Plazas are traditional open spaces in various cities around the world. They often serve as important gathering places for the community. Plazas can be found between or surrounded by buildings, and their shapes and designs can vary greatly. Some may have no vegetation, while others are lush and green. The design of a plaza is crucial, as factors such as shade and seating can greatly impact the comfort of visitors. Public art is often found in plazas, which can spark differing opinions on its aesthetic value. In recent years, there has been a rise in privately owned plazas, which have been criticized by some for being exclusive and lacking character (Byrne and Sipe, 2010).

Urban trails/ Greenways are linear pathways that are used for various recreational activities such as walking, cycling, jogging, skating, and horseback riding. These trails can be intentionally designed or repurposed from disused rail or power transmission corridors. They can have paved surfaces, but unpaved trails are also common, especially in older or rural areas. Urban trails can be found in diverse landscapes, including floodplains, woodlands, and residential areas. They are often referred to as greenways, bikeways, or hiking trails, depending on their specific design and purpose. (Byrne and Sipe, 2010).

Streets in urban areas are classified into different types, ranging from small alleys to large expressways. While many of these streets are primarily used for transportation and may not be considered open spaces, there are some exceptions. Streets like boulevards, lanes, and pedestrian-only malls can serve purposes beyond just facilitating traffic flow. For example, Strøget in Copenhagen, which became a pedestrian-only street in 1962, is known as the longest of its kind in the world and is a beloved public space in the city. Similarly, Rua XV de Novembro in Curitiba, also known as flower street, was transformed into a pedestrian mall in 1972 and has gained recognition as a well-designed civic space. In Australian cities, there are pedestrian malls like Hay Street Mall in Perth, Rundle Street Mall in Adelaide, Bourke Street Mall in Melbourne, and Queen Street Mall in Brisbane, although they are shorter in length. These pedestrian malls are mainly used for leisure activities such as walking, shopping, and people-watching (Byrne and Sipe, 2010).

UPD or Urban planning and design is seen as a collaborative, multi-disciplinary effort that involves various sectors of the city, each with its own interests in the final plan and design (Philippine Department of Human Settlements and Urban Development, 2023).

Climate Risks are the product of climate and related hazards working on the vulnerability of human and natural ecosystems (Philippine Department of Human Settlements and Urban Development, 2023).

Climate impact chains are general cause-effect relations that describe how, in principle, climatic changes are expected to cause impacts on the sectors of concern; summarize the initial scoping of potential hazards, including the associated impacts of climate change and hazards, affecting the locality and the sectors within the locality-ecosystem or geographic area (Department of the Interior and Local Government, 2017).

Sustainable well-being involves considering both environmental and social factors, and focuses on meeting human needs within the limits of the planet (Sahakian et al., 2020).

Parks are classified into different types based on their size, purpose, and location. In Korea, there are 8 types of parks, including playgrounds, neighborhood parks, walking parks, local parks, urban parks, natural city parks, cemetery parks, and sports complexes, according to the Korean Park Laws or KUPL (Oh and Jeong, 2007). In the United States, parks are classified as mini-parks, school parks, neighborhood parks, district parks, city parks, regional parks, special use areas/recreational facilities, linear parks connections and natural resource areas according to the rules of the National Recreation and Park Association or NRPA(Nicholls, 2001) These classifications help to organize and categorize parks based on how they are used and what activities they offer for people to enjoy (Belkayani et al., 2018).

Mini-parks are specialized facilities designed to cater to specific age groups with a limited population and a single function. They typically include play equipment, seating areas, and picnic tables, and are usually within a radius of 402.336 meters (1/4 miles) with sizes ranging from 232 sqm to 4043 sqm. These parks can be used for active or passive purposes and are suggested to have amenities such as a small kiosk, picnic area, benches, and a 6-foot wide track. It is advised not to park off the street near mini-parks (Belkayani et al., 2018).

School parks, with a range of sizes, are not exclusively for school use and may also fall under other classifications depending on the conditions within their boundaries (Belkayani et al., 2018).

Neighborhood parks cater to different age groups within a specific area or neighborhood, typically ranging from 20.234 m2 to 40.468 m2 in size. These parks primarily serve residents within a radius of 402.336- 804.672 m (1/4-1/2 miles) and offer a mix of active recreational areas like sports fields and playgrounds, as well as passive areas for walking, viewing, and relaxation, including picnic spots. Some neighborhood parks may also feature sports conditioning areas, serving the wider community (Belkayani et al., 2018).

District parks, which are larger than neighborhood parks and serve multiple neighborhoods, have varying sizes ranging from 121.405 sqm to 202.342 sqm. They typically cater to residents living within a radius of 1609.3443218.688 m (1/2-3 miles) and offer a range of recreational activities such as competitions, swimming, tennis, games, and volleyball. Additionally, they provide opportunities for passive recreation like walking, photography, sitting, and picnicking. In some cases, these parks may be dedicated to a specific sport, regardless of their size (Belkayani et al., 2018).

City Parks are extensive park areas that benefit the community. They typically cover a minimum size of 202.342 sqm to 303.514 sqm and cater to the entire city, offering natural or developed spaces for a variety of outdoor recreational activities including playgrounds, boating areas, fishing spots, swimming areas, picnic areas, and designated areas for adventurous games (Belkayani et al., 2018).

Regional Parks are expansive public spaces that cater to a diverse range of communities within a specific region. These parks, which are at least 500 decares in size, offer a multitude of recreational opportunities for people of all ages, both active and passive. Additionally, they may include nature preserves, wildlife habitats, and conservation

areas for educational purposes. The guidelines for regional parks, as outlined by the National Recreation and Park Association or NRPA, are flexible and can be adapted to suit the unique characteristics and resources of each area. (Belkayani et al., 2018).

Private Parks/Recreational Facilities cater to specialized or single-purpose recreation activities. According to NRPA, these parks encompass historical areas, nature centers, marinas, golf courses, zoos, conservatories, arboretums, arenas, amphitheaters, plazas, or squares. As each area is unique, there are no standard sizes or measurements (Belkayani et al., 2018).

Linear Parks and Connections are natural corridors and links that connect parks and are typically designed for recreational activities such as walking, running, cycling, skating, riding, and canoeing. Additionally, linear parks may include active playgrounds. NRPA has not established specific standards for linear parks but emphasizes the importance of protecting resources and ensuring maximum utilization (Belkayani et al., 2018).

Natural Resource Areas are designated as areas with natural and cultural significance, where recreational activities are considered a secondary purpose. Visitors are encouraged to engage in passive recreational activities such as observing wildlife and nature. The size and measurement standards for these areas are not specified, but the primary goal is to protect resources and ensure they are suitable for appropriate use (Belkayani et al., 2018).

Additional terms related to this guide can be found in Forest Foundation Philippines. (2022, March 14). Public Parks and Open Green Spaces Guidebook - Forest Foundation Philippines. https://www.forestfoundation.ph/publications/ public-parks-and-open-green-spaces-guidebook/

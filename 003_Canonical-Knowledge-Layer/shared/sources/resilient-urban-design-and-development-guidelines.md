###### Message from the Secretary

![image 8](<Resilient Urban Design and Development Guidelines_images/imageFile8.png>)

The Philippines continues to experience intensifying climate hazards and disaster risks, causing substantial damage to human settlements and the natural environment. To make development resilient, cities and towns must be better prepared to plan and implement adaptation and mitigation measures for a better, safe, and inclusive future for all Filipinos.

As the primary national government entity responsible for the management of housing, human settlement, and urban development in the country, I am pleased to present the Department of Human Settlement and Urban Development’s (DHSUD) Resilient Urban Design and Development Guidelines. This Guidelines aims to strengthen the capacities of state actors to undertake resilient urban planning and design, improve access to housing, and enhance infrastructure to withstand natural disasters and climate change impacts. This Guidelines will equip urban planners with design-anchored tools and strategies to make the urban design process more participatory and easier to adopt within existing planning frameworks.

This policy is part of the DHSUD’s PLANADO (Plan & Do) Program, which builds the capacity of local government units (LGUs) and other urban actors to formulate and implement land use, physical, and urban development plans and programs. The DHSUD calls on the support of local governments and other urban actors to promote the use of this Guidelines to achieve our shared vision of a more prosperous and resilient Philippines.

###### Foreword

The 2024 World Risk Report ranked the Philippines first among countries with the highest disaster risk due to diverse exposures, high intensities, and higher vulnerabilities, highlighting the limited coping and adaptive capacities that amplify the negative effects of natural climate-risk factors on the country’s development.

The impacts of disasters, climate change, and the recent pandemic necessitate the development and implementation of green and resilient urban design strategies such as compact, mixed-use, and transit-oriented development to improve resilience, livability, and sustainability. These interventions can be facilitated through CLUP and ZO.

While the DHSUD has directed LGUs to integrate climate-resilient measures into land use and urban design through evidence-based planning, the lack of country-specific urban planning standards and accessible tools impedes their ability to develop and implement resilient urban design strategies. Thus, DHSUD has updated its urban design and development guidelines to strengthen resilience in land use planning and provide LGUs with a practical and comprehensive guide on urban development.

The DHSUD Resilient Urban Design and Development Guidelines offers technical and policy guidance to effectively mainstream urban design into local land use planning and budgeting. It presents various urban design concepts and strategies in a simplified, illustrated format to enhance understanding and appreciation of the role of urban design in managing physical development, boosting local economic activities, and creating livable neighborhoods through quality public spaces and facilities. Moreover, the Guidelines also include urban design templates, as well as program and project ideas for LGUs to adopt and implement in their locality, considering their specific needs and development goals.

Developed with the support of the Government of Japan, Global Facility for Disaster Reduction and Recovery, and the World Bank, this Guidelines incorporates inputs from extensive consultations conducted with various urban planners, design professionals, the academe, and technical staff from other government agencies and legislative bodies.

Through the use of this Guidelines, DHSUD will strive to work with all sectors of society towards building better, greener, and smarter human settlements and urban systems in the Philippines.

###### A. BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE

Urban areas in the Philippines face various challenges, some of which are intensified by the impacts of natural disaster and climate change. Rapid urbanization has resulted in significant demographic shifts, with more than half of the population now residing in cities—a figure projected to rise to 84% by 2050.1 This rapid growth had led to sprawling of metropolitan areas such as Metro Manila, where infrastructure, health, environmental, and social systems struggle to keep pace with the growing urban population. Challenges such as persistent traffic congestion, prevailing informal settlements, and heightened disaster vulnerability are exacerbated by the country’s increasing exposure to natural hazards, which are becoming more frequent and intense due to climate change.

The urban poor are particularly affected, often lacking access to land and adequate housing which increases their susceptibility to various impacts of extreme weather events. The Asian Development Bank (ADB) emphasizes the need of building urban climate change resilience through adaptation, mitigation actions, and disaster risk reduction to address the complex challenges of these rapidly growing urban areas.2

Moreover, the urban heat island effect significantly influences temperature indices in the Philippines, with urban areas experiencing more pronounced warming trends than rural areas. This not only affects the comfort and health of residents but also contributes to the increase of energy demands for cooling, further straining the already inadequate infrastructure. The archipelagic geography of the Philippines poses additional challenges for connectivity and urban management, as many cities are located in coastal areas vulnerable to sea level rise. This vulnerability is further compounded by the variability and intensity of rainfall, which affects food security and population safety.

To address these multifaceted issues, a comprehensive and coordinated approach is required, integrating the efforts of government, the private sector, and communities. Strategies must be inclusive and sustainable, focusing on strengthening capacities for resilient urban planning and design, improving access to housing, and enhancing infrastructure to withstand the impacts of disasters and climate change. Building resilience among the urban poor is crucial, as they are the most affected by disasters and climate change, with temperatures in the Philippines projected to rise significantly in the coming decades.

The impact of disasters and climate change, population growth vis-a-vis spatial development allocations in land use—coupled with lessons learned from the global pandemic—has highlighted the need to revisit current mindsets and requirements for settlements growth, particularly through the lens of urban design as outlined in the CLUP and ZO guidebooks. To this end, the DHSUD is undertaking an initiative to develop, update, and refine its urban design guidelines. This initiative aims not only to strengthen the role of land use planning in enhancing resilience (Figure 2), but also to explore ways of harnessing urban design as a tangible binding agent that amplifies CLUP thrusts and better connect with other key planning programs and mandates.

INTRODUCTION I 5

Urban design refers to an organized spatial development process that aims to connect the socioecological history of a place with people’s activities and environmental services. This process can be achieved by shaping the physical features of cities, municipalities, barangays, and communities—whether it is for residential, commercial, industrial, and/or parks and recreation purposes—and is guided by the ZO, deeds of restrictions, and other local urban design codes.

Urban design also serves as a tool for providing specific programs and design strategies for the public and private properties, addressing challenges within the component of the CLUP, such as environmental, social, institutional, and physical. These strategies promote holistic approaches through stakeholder engagement and through leveraging a multifunctional approach that optimizes the use of financial and other resources.

To better appreciate the role of urban design in urban-regional development, it is essential to compare it with urban planning, particularly in terms of function and scale. Whileurban planning is known as a participative and consultative discipline focused on establishing medium to long-term spatial and sectoral policies and strategies, urban design focuses more on the short-term timeframes (i.e., less than five years) to implement technical design recommendations (Figure 3).

Although there is a clear distinction between urban design and urban planning, both practices remain interrelated with each other. Urban planning sets the strategic framework, while urban design translates these plans into tangible forms by shaping public spaces and features. Together, they contribute to the creation of vibrant, functional cities. By understanding urban design as the physical manifestation of urban-regional policies at the neighborhood and community level, this highlights the convergence of development aspirations that were once separated by function and scale.

###### A. BENEFITS

Urban design can significantly influence the morphology of urban form by addressing socio-environmental concerns that affect human behavior and activities. By resolving urban design-related issues and challenges, the following benefits can be realized:

- 1. Climate Resilience Climate change and global warming have exacerbated the occurrence of disasters and climate-induced environmental degradation in urban areas, such as severe flooding, typhoons, and heat island accumulation. Urban design can mitigate these challenges through a variety of spatial growth strategies, such as creating networks of compact, smart vertical developments that preserve and require open spaces. These spaces can serve as flood retention areas and a means to address heat gain, while being surrounded by smart structures that harvest solar energy and collect rainwater, thereby reducing the to lessen area’s collective carbon and environmental footprint.
- 2. Disaster Resilience Well-integrated buildings and open spaces are designed to adapt to short- and long-term emergencies, including pandemics, earthquakes, and fires by providing evacuation corridors and safe zones during and after such events.
- 3. Health and Well-being Cohesive and inclusive built environment designs encourage active lifestyle and communal activities, while considering environmental factors that adversely affect health, such as air, noise, as well as visual pollution. Improved environmental conditions and accessible networks of open spaces support livelihood, enhance sensorial experience, and promote overall well-being.
- 4. Active Transportation and Alternative Mobility Urban design that creates and facilitate various modes of transportation and mobility (e.g., walking, bicycling, public and mass transportation) reduces dependence on private vehicles as a means of accessibility, promotes healthier lifestyle, and enhance collective resilience to natural and human-induced disasters due to the mobility choices available to move people and services around different areas in and outside the city or municipality.
- 5. Energy Efficiency The synergy of urban form, shape, texture, and building massing leverages free energy and passive design strategies, lowering energy consumption for cooling buildings and lessening the strain on power supplies.
- 6. Environment and Ecology Integrating biotic and abiotic elements of nature with the built environment supports life-friendly processes for humans and other organisms, contributing to improved ecological balance and environmental service for neighborhoods and communities.
- 7. History, Culture, and Art Urban design that expresses and celebrates local values, culture, and identity enhances the distinct character of a place, fostering uniqueness and competitive advantage. Local identity features often captured through art serve as catalysts for communal interaction, inclusive development, and preservation of cultural values for the future generations of residents and visitors.
- 8. Social Equity Urban design that ensures equal access to social, physical, and environmental infrastructure within walkable distances for all households regardless of ethnicity, income class, gender, age, or physical capability.
- 9. Crime Prevention Proper design and effective use of the built environment promote community safety and security, reduce crime incidence and fear, and enhance the quality of life.

- 3. Sustainable Drainage Systems (SuDS)11 offers an alternative to conventional drainage solutions that directly channel surface water through networks of pipes and sewers to nearby watercourses. By mimicking natural drainage processes, SuDS helps to reduce surface water flooding, improve water quality, and enhance the amenity and biodiversity value of the environment.

For context, it is known that in natural environments, rain falls on permeable surfaces and soaks into the ground; a process called infiltration. In urban areas, however, where many surfaces are sealed by buildings and paving, natural infiltration is limited. Instead, drainage networks consisting of pipes and culverts divert surface water to local water courses. In some cases, this has resulted in downstream flooding and deterioration in river water quality. This is caused when foul sewers are overwhelmed by surface water, leading to the release of dirty water into rivers.

SuDS aims to mitigate these issues through the following approaches:

- • Promoting source control methods – reducing the volume of water entering the drainage/river network by intercepting run-off water on roofs for subsequent re-use (e.g., rainwater harvesting for irrigation) or for storage and subsequent evapotranspiration (e.g., green roofs).
- • Pre-treatment steps – using vegetated swales or filter trenches to remove pollutants from surface water prior to discharge to watercourses or aquifers.
- • Retention systems – delaying surface water discharge to watercourses through storage solutions, such as ponds, retention basins, or wetlands, for example.
- • Infiltration systems – mimicking natural/ground water recharge through infiltration trenches and soakways, allow water to soak into the ground.

###### B. PLACEMAKING

Placemaking refers to a collaborative and participatory process that enables communities to shape their public realm to maximize its shared value. It is a multifaceted approach in planning, design, and the management of public spaces. More than just promoting better urban design, placemaking empowers communities to collectively reimagine and reinvent public spaces12 into vibrant, inclusive, and functional environments.

To effectively transform public spaces into vibrant communities, the placemaking process follows these key principles:13

- 1. Community is the expert – The local communities hold valuable insights into how the area functions, its history, and the issues that matter most. Their perspective helps shape meaningful and effective public spaces.
- 2. Create a place, not a design – It is essential to create an environment that has both a strong sense of community, comfort, and engagement by integrating diverse activities and settings that contribute to a greater whole.
- 3. You can’t do it alone – Partnerships with schools, local institutions, and civil society organizations, etc., provide crucial support for project development and long-term success.
- 4. You can see a lot just by observing – Continuously monitoring how people use a space offers valuable insight for improving the functionality and management of these spaces over time.
- 5. Have a vision – A shared vision helps the community define its ideal public place, shaping the activities and character; more importantly, this should instill pride in the people of the community.
- 6. Start with short-term – It is suggested to start with short-term improvements that can be refined and improved over the years, such as public seating, public arts, pedestrian crosswalks, community garden, alfresco, and the like.
- 7. Triangulate – Whether different elements are arranged in relation to each other in a public space, this may provide linkage between people in the community and prompt them to interact with each other.
- 8. They always say, “it can’t be done” – Overcoming obstacles may begin with small-scale community improvements, which may demonstrate the importance of creating vibrant and valuable public spaces.

- 9. Form supports function – The design of public spaces is and should be reinforced with community input and aligned with the functional understanding of overcoming obstacle/s, as these will guide the design form to fulfill the vision of the community.

###### D. SUSTAINABLE URBAN MOBILITY

Sustainable urban mobility refers to the use of environmentally friendly transportation modes and an approach to urban development planning with sustainability goals in mind.19 It aims to establish transport systems that minimize negative impacts on the environment while promoting and enhancing accessibility, safety, and the overall quality of life for urban residents. These systems may include public transportation networks (such as buses and trains), active transportation modes (like walking and cycling), and emerging innovations like electric vehicles and micromobility options. By prioritizing sustainable mobility, cities can reduce emissions, improve air quality, and promote equitable access to transportation.

Some of the urban design strategies to achieve sustainable urban mobility may include the following:

- 1. Transit-Oriented Development (TOD)20 refers to urban planning that optimizes the availability of residential, commercial, and recreational spaces within walking distance of public transportation. In general, TOD strategically utilizes land within a one-kilometer radius of high-quality mass transit stations to create a diverse and well-balanced mix of businesses, housing, civic amenities, and leisure activities. This approach promotes adequate population density, diverse activity, and pedestrian connectivity to encourage substantial transit ridership.

A TOD Area typically exhibits the following characteristics:

- a. Centered around high-quality transit systems - ensuring seamless access to and transfers between public transportation (e.g., rail, bus rapid transit, inter-modal passenger terminals, bus interchanges, and other transport infrastructure).
- b. Compact development – high-density development with diverse activities within a given piece of land. Higher density in land use brings a critical mass of residents and workers in the TOD area, creating vibrant places.
- c. Mixed-use development – a mixture of residential, commercial, office, institutional, and recreational land uses, which can reduce the number of trips and trip lengths.
- d. Pedestrian-friendly – sidewalks and crosswalks are well-maintained, streets and well-lights, and it maintains a sense of safety among pedestrians.
- e. Seamless flow of pedestrians and motorists – seamless intermodal transport connections providing choices in transportation modes going to different destinations.
- f. Open and green spaces – provide an area for physical activity, community and civic activities, and other recreational uses to encourage walking and transit trips in the TOD area.
- g. Sense of place – fostering distinct character and culture to create a positive experience for communities and a sense of community for residents.

TOD seeks to align urban development with mass transit systems to achieve social, economic, and environmental goals and objectives. By fostering a series of interconnected Planned Unit Developments (PUD) anchored along a transit corridor, TOD enhances access to employment opportunities and essential services, promotes affordable housing, and supports heritage conservation efforts. Economically, TOD reduces transportation costs, increases property values, and fosters efficient space utilization. Environmentally, it mitigates reliance on fossil fuels, curbs urban sprawl, and preserves/conserves green spaces. Ultimately, TOD improves urban accessibility and enhances land values, thus enabling the use of Land Value Capture (LVC) as a funding mechanism for sustainable urban development projects.

Complete Streets is an approach to planning, designing, building, operating, and maintaining streets that enables safe access for all users, including pedestrians, bicyclists, motorists, and public transit riders of all ages and abilities.21 This approach prioritizes inclusivity, particularly for individuals who have experienced systemic underinvestment, such as older adults, persons with disabilities, or those without access to private vehicles (Figure 29). While there is no single formula for complete streets, it is necessary to lay down several key elements that define their effectiveness:

- a. Pedestrian Facilities – This includes adequate and unobstructed walking space, lighting fixtures, seating, shading, roadway separation, street trees and other softscaping, and safe, frequent at-grade crossings.
- b. Bicycle Facilities – Essential components include designated bike lanes, secure bike parking, intersection treatments, and accessible destinations for cyclists.

###### F. HERITAGE CONSERVATION AND CULTURE

Heritage Conservation and Culture refers to the actions or processes undertaken to safeguard the character-defining elements of a cultural resource, ensuring the retention of its heritage value and the extension of its physical life. It does not imply freezing a structure in time, converting into a museum or restricting property owners from making any modifications or changes with their properties. Rather, heritage conservation aims to maintain or preserve and enhance the value of historic buildings by maintaining their original built-form and architectural elements. This approach prioritizes restoration over replacement or demolition, ensuring that significant architectural features are retained. When restoration is not feasible, heritage conservation encourages the respectfully recreation of the scale, period, and character of the structure to maintain its historical integrity within a modern context.

Before integrating the UDD process with the CLUP process, it is essential to determine which scenario applies to the LGUs. Identifying the appropriate scenario helps pinpoint or distinguish the appropriate step wherein the LGUs can start in the UDD process, guiding the selection of suitable approaches, tools, and project ideas that may be utilized for the Local Urban Design Guide and in choosing project ideas during design workshops.

- Scenario 1: Land uses and zonal areas and regulations are already implemented and simply being enhanced, reinforced in the new CLUP. LGUs implementing land use policies as stated in the new CLUP can implement urban design projects to further enhance and revitalize said land use zone/s as areas for redevelopment (Step 11 – UDD process).

In this case, the UDD Technical Working Group should focus on the following:

- • Step 1- UDD Process: Enforcing and monitoring zoning regulations;
- • Step 7, 8, 9, 10 – UDD Process: Amending or supplementing zoning regulations and updating the list of projects in investment programs based on the results of implementations; and
- • Step 11 – UDD Process: Communicating the Local Urban Design Guidelines to stakeholders and the general public to broaden awareness and maximize the positive impact of urban design policies, plans, and projects.

- Scenario 2: LGUs with no CLUP or for updating. For LGUs that lack a CLUP or require an update, urban design can help reinforce the new direction of the revised CLUP by further assessing the effectiveness and efficiency of the previous CLUP or ZO. In this scenario:

- • New design guidelines should be developed to support zonal and form-based code regulations that are responsive to the needs and collective aspirations of stakeholders.
- • It is recommended that these LGUs undergo the entire UDD process, starting from Step 1, to ensure a comprehensive integration of urban design into the new CLUP.

###### STEP 1: ORGANIZE URBAN DESIGN AND DEVELOPMENT TECHNICAL WORKING GROUP (UDD-TWG)

It is recommended that as early as Step 1 (Organize) of the land use planning process, efforts to organize an Urban Design & Development - Technical Working Group (UDD-TWG) be constituted as well; coinciding with the identification of stakeholder groups who will serve as primary anchor in acquiring policy thrusts and development strategy inputs for succeeding steps in the land use planning process.

The UDD-TWG group may be composed of the following members:

- a. Local Planning and Development Office - One (1) member. Envisioned to serve as team leader so that urban design strategies and thrusts to be created will be aligned with existing as well as upcoming urban planning policies and plans, as well as to achieve greater synergy in terms of UDD-CLUP integration.
- b. Local Architect and/or Local Engineer - One (1) to Two (2) members. Will assist by providing valuable physical planning expertise in close coordination with other technical experts who are also members of this TWG.
- c. Local Zoning Officer - One (1) member. It would be ideal that the Zoning Officer be part of the TWG to achieve greater synergy in the updating and refinement of the LGUs ZO, and its eventual implementation and enforcement.
- d. Local Housing Officer - One (1) member. With adequate housing as a major component in achieving equity and social justice in urban development, it is recommended that this office be a key component of the TWG; to ensure that safe, affordable, resilient and adaptive housing provisions be reflected in the urban design program of the city or municipality.
- e. Private Sector - One (1) to Two (2) members.
- f. Professional Organization (UAP, PALA, PIEP) - One to Two (1-2) representatives from each Professional Organization.
- g. Academe - One (1) representatives from each institution. To provide historical and research driven inputs which can further refine the TWGs outputs.

The members of this TWG will attend and/or participate in the CLUP workshop proceedings. If there is a concurrent CLUP workshop, it is recommended that the UDD-TWG be divided into two (2) sub-groups. This ensures that both CLUP and CDP workshops have UDD-TWG representation, allowing emerging themes and strategies into the UDD of the city or municipality.

###### STEP 2: REVIEW, ALIGNMENT WITH VISION, GOALS AND OBJECTIVES IN THE CLUP

It is essential to review the CLUP vision and goals of the LGU to ensure that urban design and development concepts and strategies are aligned and complementary from the outset of the planning process. The city or municipality’s vision statement may be refined or updated, if necessary, to better integrate urban design principles. Any updates or revisions should be presented to local governing bodies to ensure alignment with the LGU’s development policies and to secure the necessary support and endorsement.

Additionally, higher-level plans—such as the National Physical Framework Plan (NPFP), Medium-Term Philippine Development Plan (MTPDP), Regional Physical Framework Plan (RPFP), Regional Development Plan (RDP), and Provincial Development and Physical Framework Plan (PDPFP)—should be reviewed. This will help identify the LGU’s possible role and contributions to the development thrusts and goals of its region or area. This will also provide insights into urban development projects that may be initiated by the national or provincial government, allowing the LGU to align its strategies and maximize opportunities for coordinated development.

###### STEP 3: ASSESSMENT OF ENVIRONMENTAL CONTEXT

A comprehensive understanding of the overall environmental context for designing a distinct and resilient urban environment. Equally important is the assessment of climate change and disaster vulnerability, as these factors play a key or significant role in shaping development decisions. By thoroughly analyzing these factors, planners can better determine the suitability of development and strategically position it within the broader environmental and risk landscape.50

At this stage of the land use planning process (i.e., CLUP Step 4 – Analyze the Situation), the Ecological Profile (EP) and the Climate and Disaster Risk Assessment (CDRA) serve as the primary source of information. Urban design-related assessments should be anchored on these sources or documents to ensure a context-sensitive approach. Additionally, a detailed review of CDP, other thematic plans of the LGU, and those of adjacent localities is also essential for a more comprehensive understanding of the LGU’s development context.

In this regard, a preliminary technical assessment at the city or municipal scale may now be made. Some of the data requirements are (but not limited to) the following:

Integrating resilient urban design with sustainable land use planning enables cities and municipalities to create more connected, inclusive, and empowering environments that safeguard the well-being of residents and workers. A comprehensive understanding of the urban context allows for the strategic positioning of developments, maximizing their impact and long-term value.

###### STEP 4: IDENTIFY URBAN DESIGN STRATEGIES BASED ON CLUP DEVELOPMENT THRUST

At this step or stage, the local government must identify, select, and adopt the appropriate urban design strategies that are aligned and consistent with the development thrusts outlined in their CLUP. These strategies should be composed of coordinated actions designed to address specific urban challenges or achieve a targeted development goal and/ or outcome.

These Guidelines emphasize the importance of the use of urban design strategies and interventions in addressing or responding to natural hazards. As a starting point, LGUs may refer to the list of urban design strategies presented in Table 2. The LGU can also identify additional strategies that can be considered and incorporated to ensure a more appropriate or tailored and effective approach.

###### STEP 6: VERIFICATION

At this stage, priority areas within the LGU have been identified in the previous step, allowing for a more site-specific survey of the selected site/s and or location/s to be conducted. The initial data gathered in the earlier steps will be verified, and any gaps will be addressed through on-site visits and primary data collection methods.

It is important to emphasize that the technical assessment conducted and discussed in Step 3 was broader in scale, focusing on the overall appreciation of the city or municipality’s existing conditions. In contrast, this step would also involve a detailed and focused assessment of the identified priority areas.

By using the EP as an initial data source or baseline dataset, along with preliminary technical assessment from Step 3, LGUs can optimize primary data gathering or collection efforts and minimize unnecessary logistical requirements. Site-specific data gathering and surveys should focus on the scope or information gaps not adequately identified and addressed in the EP or in other aspects during the initial UDD discussions and design strategy development. Based on these findings, a site-specific analysis will be conducted in order to validate, refine, and further develop the UDD strategies.

Some of the key points to be assessed are (but not limited to) the following:

- 1. Sun path and wind direction (related to the site/s identified) To determine and optimize building orientation and maximize sun shading and natural lighting, it is essential to conduct a thorough analysis of the sun’s path and prevailing wind patterns. Proper orientation of the structure or building maximized natural lighting, ensuring effective sun shading and passive cooling for a certain development project.

Additionally, understanding the wind flow dynamics would enhance air circulation and natural ventilation. This comprehensive analysis can result to a more sustainable and comfortable urban environments or structures.

###### STEP 8: CONDUCT OF DESIGN WORKSHOP

After the creation of UDD conceptual plan and design—based on the TWG’s site surveys of identified priority areas — it is essential to organize a design workshop. This workshop will serve as a platform to present the initial UDD strategies, plans, and designs to relevant key stakeholders and resilience actors. Many of these individuals are represented and were able to provide insights during the CLUP workshops, which guided and inspired the TWG’s approach.

A one (1)-day workshop is ideal, allowing sufficient time for the UDD-TWG to present its concepts and for stakeholders to provide immediate feedback afterward. If multiple UDD priority areas exist, creating breakout sessions should be considered to enable or facilitate concurrent presentations and more focused discussions. Proper documentation of all inputs and feedbacks gathered from the conducted workshops is crucial. This ensures that, in cases of contestation after UDD finalization, the TWG can defend and highlight its approach and/or process as well as highlight the inclusive and participatory process undertaken. Suggested stakeholders for these workshops include, but are not limited to the following groups:

- a. Academe – academic institutions, such as colleges and universities offering programs in architecture, engineering, urban planning, and community development, that are familiar with the area may be invited to provide theoretical and research-based insights.
- b. Civil Society Organizations - local non-government organizations (NGOs) and non-profit groups, including farmers and fisherfolk associations, vendors, urban poor representatives, youth groups, seniors, persons with disabilities (PWDs), LGBTQ+ community organizations, as well as heritage and cultural development groups, should be invited to ensure inclusive representation in the formulation of the UDD projects in the city or municipality. Additionally, affected or concerned homeowners’ association should be considered for participation to integrate community perspective into the finalization of the UDD initiatives.
- c. Government Offices - representatives from local, provincial and regional representatives of national government agencies (NGAs) (e.g., Department of Internal and Local Government (DILG), Department of Environment and Natural Resources (DENR), Department of Education (DepEd), Department of Transportation (DoTR), Department of Social Welfare and Development (DSWD), Department of Health (DOH), etc.) should be invited to ensure policy and plan alignment. It is also essential that other key offices in the LGU, such as the urban poor and tourism offices, should also participate enhance intra-LGU program integration within the LGU.
- d. Private Sector Groups – potential investors, real estate groups or developers, and other private sector stakeholders should be invited to provide their perspectives and/or insights on the proposed urban design projects, ensuring that economic viability and investment opportunities are considered.
- e. Community Groups – it is also important to invite community groups and homeowners’ associations to ensure that their opinions towards the proposed projects will be heard and integrated into the final revision of the UDD project.

###### STEP 10: INTEGRATION INTO THE CLUP AND ZONING ORDINANCE

The refined and detailed UDD conceptual plan and design can now be integrated into the final draft of the CLUP and, more importantly, into the drafting of the ZO. These refined outputs serve as critically important inputs that should be considered in the preparation of the CLUP’s final land use map, which indicates the allocation of existing and proposed land uses. Moreover, the UDD planning team may also identify necessary policies that will facilitate the enforcement of specific land and water uses in the city or municipality, which coincide with the priority UDD areas and strategies earlier identified. Examples of such policies include conservation, preservation, maintenance, and development of ecological systems, cultural heritage sites, natural resources, and the like; CCA and DRR land use policies such as delineation of no-build zones or buffer zones; as well as incentives, both fiscal and non-fiscal (i.e. development rights, streamlined permitting processes), to achieve the desired development objective for special development areas or growth areas.

By using the outputs of the previous steps, there will be a more seamless transition or integration of this information towards developing a more Form-based zoning strategy that regulates as well as proactively establishes development guidelines in both individual and collective building scales, and intra and inter-LGU connections and urban fabric cohesiveness.

To further expound, the UDD conceptual outputs emanating from previous steps would be used as sources and references to be detailed in the drafting of the Form-based Code narrative details. The contents of Figure 77 - Figure 81 are examples of concepts, forms, and design themes that are to be detailed in the creation of a more performanceoriented or form-based ZO.

Form-based zoning is a regulatory approach that emphasizes the physical form of the built environment rather than the separation of land uses. This method aims to create predictable and high-quality public spaces by focusing on the relationship between building facades and the public realm, the form and mass of buildings, and the scale and types of streets and blocks.

It would be ideal that UDD priority areas would comprise projects representing most, if not all, zonal areas in a city or municipality. By using RUDD Steps 7 and 9 outputs, which would be detailed in RUDD Step 10, better CLUP and UDD integration would be realized. For instances where there be zonal areas not specified in Step 9, ZO proponents will already have reference forms (building massing and block orientation cues) to anchor said Form-based zoning strategies. Major programs and projects identified in priority UDD areas should also be incorporated as part of the overall land use plan.

It is worth noting that, should there be a need to amend the existing ZO to incorporate the conceptual plans and designs formulated in the previous steps, it is vital that the Local Zoning Review Committee (LZRC) be involved. It is the inherent function of the LZRC to determine the amendments or revisions necessary in the ZO brought about by the changes to be introduced in the CLUP.

To the extent possible, inclusion of preliminary or indicative costings for these UDD projects is highly recommended since this will make said UDD projects more easily integrated in the Local Development Investment Program of the LGU, a step closer towards funding and implementation. Alternatively, as the case may be, a more detailed UDD package (refer to Annex B) can be more easily presented and discussed with potential Public-Private Partnerships (PPP) investors or partners.

###### Defined

Form-based zoning is a modern approach to urban planning that focuses on the physical form of the built environment rather than the separation of uses, which is typical in conventional zoning. This method emphasizes the design of buildings, streets, and public spaces to create a specific urban form and character. It aims to foster predictable built results and high-quality public realms by using physical form as the organizing principle. The regulations in form-based codes are detailed in both text and visuals, ensuring clarity and ease of implementation. They are often adopted into city or town law, offering a structured alternative to conventional zoning regulations. Form-based zoning is not just about controlling land use; it’s about shaping the public space to enhance community interaction and walkability, reflecting a community’s vision for its future.

###### Jurong Lake District, Singapore

In Singapore, the form-based zoning approach is being explored to enhance the walkability and livability of neighborhoods, particularly in high-density areas like Housing and Development Board (HDB) estates. The Jurong Lake District (JLD) in Singapore is a prime example of form-based zoning in action. This approach emphasizes the physical form and design of the built environment to create a vibrant, sustainable, and livable urban area. Based on its Urban Planning and Design Guide (2023), some of its key aspects includes:

- 1. Mixed-Use Development: JLD integrates residential, commercial, and recreational spaces to create a dynamic environment where people can live, work, and play. This mix of uses is designed to reduce the need for long commutes and encourage a more sustainable lifestyle.
- 2. Pedestrian-Friendly Design: The district is planned with a focus on walkability. This includes wide sidewalks, pedestrian pathways, and green spaces that make it easy and pleasant to move around on foot.
- 3. Green and Blue Spaces: JLD features extensive green spaces and water bodies, including parks and the Jurong Lake itself. These elements not only enhance the aesthetic appeal but also contribute to environmental sustainability and residents’ well-being.
- 4. Public Transport Integration: The district is well-connected by public transport, including MRT lines and bus services. This integration supports a car-lite lifestyle and reduces traffic congestion.
- 5. Sustainable Building Practices: Buildings in JLD are designed with sustainability in mind, incorporating energy-efficient technologies and green building standards.

###### STEP 11: IMPLEMENTATION OF URBAN DESIGN GUIDELINES AND PROJECTS

# Local Urban Design Guide

INTRODUCTION I 105

It is highly recommended that a Local Urban Design Guide be developed by the local government unit following the mainstreaming of resilient urban design and development into the CLUP/ZO. This design guide, developed by the LGUs, shall be used as a basis and reference of urban planners and designers in developing the details of their related plans and projects. It contributes to the translation of CLUP’s vision and goals through quality urban design.

Typically, Local Urban Design Guides include statutory provisions on land use, building design, public spaces, transportation, and infrastructure to create a cohesive and sustainable urban built environment. It may also contain explanations and illustrations to further demonstrate and contextualize its provisions.

The content of the Local Urban Design Guide may include the following:

###### INTER-CITY REHABILITATION AND ESTABLISHMENT OF A CONNECTED OPEN SPACE MOBILITY NETWORK

- A. Project Rationale The collective Philippine experience brought about by the COVID-19 pandemic brought to light the exclusionary impact of lockdowns, particularly on public transportation. Those who had private vehicles were okay; those who depended on public utility vehicles (PUVs), which at the time were not allowed to travel, had to walk long distances to acquire needed supplies for their household. This prompted a significant part of the local population to rediscover the value of bicycles beyond that of a recreational vehicle.

Fast forward to today, efforts at establishing bike lanes via lessening vehicular lanes along major thoroughfares are being made. Although laudable, policymakers may wish to expand and capitalize on assets that, in recent years, have appeared to have lost value in the eyes of local citizens and politicians alike.

Public parks and community playgrounds, if looked at simply through their primary uses (i.e., as recreational areas), seem to be overtaken by more urgent uses such as the establishment of Barangay Halls or multi-level parking facilities. By covering-up these vital green open spaces, not only do our cities make themselves more vulnerable to being Urban Heat Islands (thus impacting unsustainable energy consumption patterns, and overall discomfort due to heat and humidity), our cities also lose evacuation areas during earthquakes, as well as porous surfaces that can hold and absorb floodwaters. As such, there is a need to broaden the role of these open space assets as functional nodes; to be part of a system of mobility corridors with rest (and responsible consumerism) nodes that when seen as a whole can provide cities with a vital system that facilitates movement of people, goods, and services may it be on a regular day, or more importantly, during times of emergencies and disasters.

- B. Project Details The conceptualization, planning and design of this system of open spaces, commonly known as Open Space Network Planning, is about building designed connections between existing parks and understanding how the spaces in between act as a connective, binding agent (Garret, J.T., n.d.); a safe, connected mobility loop that has designated rest points (i.e. parks and playgrounds) which can be further developed to host concessionaires made up of MSMEs (e.g., PUV driver groups that do not wish to have new franchises under the modernization program may be tapped to be entrepreneurs and custodians meant to maintain these) to provide service (food, drinks, etc.) to people traversing these trails.

By gradually developing and connecting parks (as mobility nodes) with a looped network of linear green strips along secondary roads (as mobility corridors), we create a development strategy that extends beyond park reach, broadens its functional value of open spaces in the city and among its inhabitants. Moreover, it is important to emphasize the necessity of understanding how this wider open space system complements the parks situated in a city; this does not relegate parks to a lesser role; it is about placing these green spaces within a larger system that enhances its collective impact at improving quality of life in cities.

- C. Risks Affecting The Project

- 1. Severe Tropical Storms – A Series of mobility corridors and nodes will provide citizens with alternative means of reaching key areas in the city they are based in; if this network goes beyond and achieves inter-city connectivity, then this open space network loop’s value to regional resilience towards storms is enhanced as well.

If land-banking strategies as entwined with this network of open spaces is achieved and supplemental infrastructure complements realized (e.g., evacuation hub network, rental housing buildings strategically placed around the city and beyond), the impact of storms as a disaster is lessened due to the ability of citizens to go about their day even if there is a tropical storm.

- 2. Flooding - Such a network of mobility corridors and nodes will provide citizens with alternative means of reaching key areas in-city, with flooding a factor, but no longer a debilitating issue as they were before. If this network goes beyond and achieves inter-city connectivity, then this open space network loop’s value to regional resilience towards flooding and overall mobility is enhanced as well.

If land-banking strategies as entwined with this network of open spaces is achieved and supplemental infrastructure complements realized (e.g., evacuation hub network, rental housing buildings strategically placed around the city and beyond), the impact of flooding as a disaster is lessened due to the ability of citizens to go about their day even if there is a tropical storm.

- 3. Earthquake - Should an earthquake strike, emergency routes and sites to set camp are required, then this network of open spaces is already in place.
- 4. Tsunami - People in need of refuge will be able to take advantage of the mobility corridors and nodes in this system to move away from vulnerable areas in the event of a tsunami; affected communities will have broader escape routes and evacuation areas to choose from. Moreover, the transportation of supplies will also be enhanced via this network of open spaces.
- 5. Fire, shootings, acts of terrorism, stampede, virus pandemic, as well as incidents of mass violence. These human-induced disasters will all benefit from having an alternative system of linear strips of green spaces, with well-designed tree-lined sidewalks and bicycle lanes that culminate in a network of open spaces (e.g., parks, playgrounds).

###### DEVELOPING A LOOP OF MULTI-STOREY RENTAL HOUSING BUILDINGS ANCHORED ON AN OPEN SPACE NETWORK OF NODES AND CORRIDORS

- A. Project Rationale In any urban renewal project, one often asks how the affected citizens’ place of residence can be taken into consideration for the duration of the renewal. Most of the time, relocation is the easiest answer; however, in cases that relocation may not be feasible (e.g., project number 2 as mentioned above), effort should be made to provide temporary accommodations within or near the city core for affected households; once the urban renewal project is done, they may return to their original areas, and move into upgraded living/working conditions.

In other instances, there may be an unfortunate fire or earthquake that ravages a community, and most of the time will entail considerable time to repair/bring back what was lost; temporary accommodations such as evacuation areas and tent cities can only do so much, better living conditions should be allocated.

Most of the time, how can local governments provide their citizens with decent, affordable housing that is close to work, yet affordable, one that does not require permanent ownership, but is rental in nature? Rental housing is normal anyway in many urban areas in the Philippines; we see varying bed space or rooms for rent signages, showing a lucrative model used by the private sector. Why not have the government make a better model, one that also factors in disaster resiliency and is a catalyst for urban revitalization?

It is under these circumstances that the value of land-banking by government is highlighted; the availability of land acquired prior to increase in land value by government, will enable it to create infrastructure opportunities that will serve those are mostly not catered to by Private Sector entities developing areas in the city (most especially high impact zones near or close to mass-transit stations).

Among the initiatives that may benefit from this land banking strategy is the creation of a rental housing network, one that is anchored on the spatial development thrusts of the previous project mentioned.

- B. Project Details As mentioned in earlier conceptual templates (urban design project ideas), the value of local governments creating or strengthening their respective Urban Renewal Authority (URA) offices will be a key factor in managing these rental housing buildings; they may consider hiring a 3rd-Party master lessee, a non-profit organization that has organizational thrusts that are aligned with what these structures stand for would be ideal.

With profit not a main consideration (thereby rental costs are made more affordable), and Communal housing or Dormitory-inspired rental housing types as inspiration, these buildings are primarily meant to provide rental housing opportunities for citizens that are not yet in a position or do not yet wish to be indebted to purchase a house or apartment to their name but may want to pursue independent housing already.

It is recommended that of all the rooms to be developed (say, a 4-storey structure at 25 rooms per floor totaling 100 rooms), 10 or more rooms will be allocated for households that will be affected by nearby Urban Renewal Projects; with the rest being offered to interested citizens who wish to live near their work, but not needing to pay very expensive monthly rates given the ideal location of the rental housing provided by said local government. These numbers, of course, will and should be validated by feasibility studies later on.

- C. Risks Affecting The Project

- 1. Severe Tropical Storms - Rental Housing buildings anchored on earlier mentioned mobility corridors and nodes will provide citizens with alternative means of living, moving about and reaching key areas in city they are based in; if this network goes beyond and achieves inter-city connectivity, then this open space network loop’s value to rental housing and resilience against storms is enhanced as well.

If land-banking strategies are entwined with these areas in which rental housing buildings are to be strategically placed, the impact of storms as a disaster is lessened due to the ability of citizens to go about their day even if there is a tropical storm.

- 2. Flooding - Such a network of rental housing buildings will provide citizens with better means of living near their place of work, with flooding a factor, but no longer a debilitating issue as they were before.
- 3. Earthquake - Should an earthquake strike, and emergency routes and sites to set camp are required, then access to these rental housing buildings, anchored on a city’s network of open spaces (as mobility corridors and nodes) are already in place.
- 4. Tsunami - People in need of refuge will be able to take advantage of the higher floors of these rental housing buildings to move away from vulnerable areas in the event of a tsunami.

- D. Land Use and Zoning Type These Residential initiatives that are rental in nature, affordable yet strategically located as is with other high-end condominium buildings and residential enclaves that will rise (or already existent) in the city may be assigned as Socialized Housing zoning and is a strong indicator of social justice in terms of providing equal opportunities and accommodations, especially for social groupings in most need of support.
- E. Benefits By enabling many of the city’s workforce to live close to their place of work, and ensuring that the city’s urban revitalization efforts can proceed with less hindrances at stake, Cities will have a more productive workforce, a more content citizenry, and a urban revitalization agenda (with urban design as development catalyst) that can proceed as planned.

These structures (partial or whole) may likewise be repurposed to serve as Evacuation Hubs during times of calamities and disasters.

###### NETWORK OF ENHANCED WAITING SHED TYPES FOR PEDESTRIANS AND MOTORCYCLISTS AT DESIGNATED PUV STOPS

- A. Project Rationale Pedestrians, commuters, and motorcycle riders are observed to be most vulnerable during sudden downpours and strong winds that accompany typhoons and tropical depressions, being stranded for hours, mostly due to accompanying flash floods en route to their homes, schools, or offices. Most of the time, people are trapped in cramped waiting sheds with no store for them to grab a quick bite, call loved ones, charge their devices, or seek cover from the rain.

Meant to be placed along key routes of the green open space network of Parkways, as discussed in earlier parts of this guideline, strategically placed enhanced waiting sheds along designated and regulated jeepney and bus stops will serve a larger role in providing relief to our pedestrians, motorists, and commuters. For one, aside from the common waiting shed typology, it is recommended that commercial stall/s be integrated into these sheds, along with acceptable waiting area facilities (e.g., lean-to rails), wifi connectivity, bicycle tire pump, among other features.

- B. Project Details A key component of this urban design idea is the rationalization of PUV routes in Philippine cities. To justify investment (also land readjustment, as necessary), PUVs must stop the decades-old practice of stopping anywhere along their franchised route. There should be predictability on where passengers embark and disembark, so that investments can be made to take advantage of the heavy foot traffic expected on these designated stops.

With regard to operation and maintenance, it is suggested that PUV franchises plying said routes be given opportunities to expand their cooperative’s collective income by having MSME shops dovetailed to these waiting sheds. This way, PUV drivers will see the wisdom on adhering to designated PUV stops (i.e., not stopping anywhere as has been the case for decades), and herd their passengers (and potential customers) into designated PUV stops with said commercial stalls connected with them.

Perhaps jeepney groups that no longer have franchises may also be invited to become entrepreneurs and provide service to the commuters that will be using these sheds daily; much more if there is a storm or flood, and they are stuck on these waiting sheds. The MSMEs manning these waiting shed stalls will also be great ‘eyes-on-the-street’ partners of law enforcement and may be tapped to observe and report to their LGU counterparts (e.g., Police, Fire Protection, Health Services.

- C. Risks Affecting the Project

- 1. Severe Tropical Storms - so that commuters will have an emergency area to wait out the heavy rains as they travel to and from work, with complementary services that can enrich the collective daily experience in these shelters.
- 2. Flooding - while waiting for floodwaters to subside, trapped commuters need not feel helpless due to the available services in the waiting sheds.

- D. Land Use and Zoning Type These transit-nodes with commercial allocations may be classified as Transportation and Services Zones if need be, or most probably these will be zoned as is the prevailing land use allocation in the area/s they are placed into.

- E. Benefits Groups that have lost their jobs, such as jeepney drivers who do not wish to align with e-jeepney franchises, may, through their consolidated groupings may be tapped to manage these stops and be entrepreneurial partners of the LGU they are part of.

These enhanced waiting shed developments can also be a vital security partner, due to reports that can be derived from the stall concessionaires in charge per shed; as mentioned earlier, a key component of this urban design idea is the rationalization of PUV routes in Philippine cities.
